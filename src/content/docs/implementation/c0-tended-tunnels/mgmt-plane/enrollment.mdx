title: Enrollment

Enrollment is what makes a host a member of a rete — and, once per rete, what makes the rete exist at all. Two acts carry the name: **genesis**, which an operator performs once on their own hardware, and **node enrollment**, repeated for every machine that joins and again whenever one is re-keyed.

This page is the design, written for whoever implements or changes enrollment. [Shape](#shape) is the map — structure, flow, and the rules that hold across both acts. The [Decision Log](#decision-log) at the foot records what was settled and what was rejected, and is where to start before changing any of it. [Evolution sketches](#evolution-sketches) names what this milestone deliberately does not build and what each of those futures needs from the design. Everything between is the reasoning that connects them.

**What a person needs in order to enroll a node is not here.** The commands in order, the delivery advice, the per-platform notes: that is the operator guide, [#14 (User guide)](https://github.com/rete-labs/florete/issues/14), and this page deliberately does not duplicate it.

## What enrollment is

**Two acts share the name, and they differ in almost everything but vocabulary.** **Genesis** happens once per rete, entirely on operator hardware, and creates the authority every later act depends on. **Node enrollment** happens for every machine that joins, is repeated whenever a node is re-keyed, and is a two-party operation across a channel Florete does not control. They belong on one page because they share a command surface and because genesis *is* enrollment — the operator's own machine joins by exactly the flow any other node uses.

**Install leaves a host ready to enroll; enroll never installs.** Everything below every rete — executables, the per-OS service wrapper — is the [installer](../../installer)'s. Everything per-rete arrives here: identity material and an initial artifact set, and nothing more — enroll starts nothing. Everything after that is the [agent](../agent)'s: activation, syncing, supervising, and owning the scope root's contents. Enroll is the single act that crosses between them, which is why it is the only thing that creates a scope root and the only thing that hands one over.

**Enrollment needs no network, and that is an invariant rather than a convenience** ([ENRL-C0-15](#decision-log)). A node that cannot reach anything comes up on what it was given and syncs when a path exists. This is the property that makes provisioning a vehicle at a depot the same operation as provisioning a server, and it must survive every later change to how bundles are delivered.

**The invariant has an operator-side half, and it is the half easiest to lose** ([ENRL-C0-37](#decision-log)). Management state reaches a node by two channels — the coordinator, online and incremental and carrying artifacts alone, and the bundle, couriered and manual and carrying identity material as well — and **neither may become a precondition of the other**. The bundle is the more capable of the two and it is the repair path when the other fails, so a rule requiring the coordinator anywhere on the bundle's path would mean the remedy for a dead coordinator needed a working one. The independence costs something and the price is stated where it is paid: two channels converge at different speeds, so a revocation takes effect at each node's next sync and a courier-fed node is the slowest ([ENRL-C0-38](#decision-log)).

## Genesis

Genesis brings a rete into existence, and it runs before Florete does — the last step is the first Florete call the rete ever makes. The sequence is the Shape's `genesis` block; what follows is why it is ordered that way.

**The repository comes first, because the rete must be named before anything is minted in its name.** `rete.name` is the SPIFFE trust domain, so it is not a label an operator revises casually; every principal's identity is scoped by it. `retectl init <name>` creates the scaffold and then the root CA and the `management-plane/primary` signer, and **it is the only way a root CA comes into existence** ([ENRL-C0-03](#decision-log)). It also writes the operator tree's own `identity/rete.json`, naming the trust domain and no node — an operator's tree belongs to a rete rather than to a machine in it — so that `retectl` reads the domain out of a record like every other holder instead of off the directory it found the tree in ([ENRL-C0-40](#decision-log)). Everything it makes afterwards is scoped by a name that already exists.

**The management node is bootstrapped before the operator's own machine**, because the coordinator has to exist before anything can publish to it. Its bundle carries `coordinator.json` alongside the rest of its artifact set, so the coordinator comes up under the agent like any other supervised workload — see [Coordinator § Run story](../coordinator#run-story). The operator's machine follows as an ordinary participant, and `retectl publish` is the first Florete-over-Florete call the rete makes. **Every step before that publish happens outside Florete, because the publish is itself a Florete call.** After it, the coordinator serves the rete's state to every node that can reach it — authoritative for distribution, never for authorship, which stays with the repository.

**Genesis needs no special case, and that is a property rather than a coincidence.** Its two bundles are cut before any coordinator exists to publish to, which under a publish-gated issuance would be the one situation needing an exemption — and an exemption written for genesis would be silently absent the day a management node has to be rebuilt, which is genesis happening again in worse circumstances. Because the gate is the commit ([ENRL-C0-35](#decision-log)), genesis is an ordinary sequence whose publish falls last: compile and commit at step 4, issue and enroll, publish when there is finally something to publish to.

**Three identities exist by the end and are never merged** ([ENRL-C0-19](#decision-log)). The **root CA** is the issuing authority and not a principal at all: it signs certificates and never envelopes, and its private key is the one thing that must never leave the machine. The **mgmt signer** is a signing principal whose key stays in operator custody and is loaded by no vertex, ever. The **operator's own identity** is an ordinary TLS principal, delivered in a bundle and revocable like anyone's — which is the merge that matters, since a signing identity must not be revocable by an HR event.

**Operator material lives in `retectl`'s own tree and never in the repository** ([ENRL-C0-06](#decision-log), [ENRL-C0-07](#decision-log)). The certificates are absent for a reason worth stating on this page: the compiler embeds every signer's certificate into the `signature` of each envelope it emits, and the compiled tree *is* committed, so a separate copy in the repository adds no fact a reviewer did not already have. See [Installer § Directories](../../installer#directories) for how the tree sits beside flor's own layout, and [CLI Surface](../cli) for what `retectl` reads from it.

**One machine holds all of it, and C0 has no way to move it** ([ENRL-C0-34](#decision-log)). A `git clone` carries the repository and nothing else — not the root key, not the signer, not `retectl`'s configuration — so the machine that ran `retectl init` is the rete's only operator machine for the life of the milestone.

`issue-bundle` assembles each node's `anchors.pem` from the operator's root certificates — one of them, in this milestone.

## The bundle

**Two flows are named throughout this design, and only the first is built.** In **Flow A** the operator generates every principal's keypair on their own machine and delivers both halves in a bundle; the private key travels. In **Flow B** the principal generates its own keypair and sends only a certificate signing request, so no private key ever leaves the machine that will hold it. **C0 is Flow A only** — during pilots the operator is the Florete team, so the trust Flow A asks for already exists in fact — and Flow B is an open question for [C1](/docs/implementation/c1-manual-mesh/scope), where its commands would be `retectl id keygen` and `retectl id sign`.

A bundle is one file per node, carrying every principal that runs on that machine — user, service, and the node itself. **It is per node rather than per principal** because a server hosting five services would otherwise mean five deliveries, five bearer files and five windows to close, for one machine that is trusted or not as a unit.

**It is a tar container, and its entry names are identifiers rather than destinations.**

```
<node>.bundle
├── manifest.json             schema_version, trust_domain and node, every entry below
│                             by name and SHA-256 digest, and a signature block over
│                             the canonical manifest
├── anchors.pem               the rete's trust anchors
├── credentials/<leaf>.crt    one per principal on the node, named by the SPIFFE
│                             leaf the certificate itself carries — a label
│                             checked against that certificate, never read as one
├── credentials/<leaf>.key    a separate entry, so a keyless bundle is expressible;
│                             matched to its certificate by public key, not by name
└── artifacts/<plane>/<name>.json   each envelope byte-exact as the compiler emitted it
```

`flor enroll` derives every path it writes, and derives all of them from content rather than from names. Artifacts it does not place at all: they are deposited in `incoming/` and **placed by the agent's intake, which reads each destination out of the envelope** ([Agent § Sync](../agent#sync)) — one rule for a set arriving from the coordinator and a set arriving from a bundle. Credentials it places itself and by the same rule: it parses each certificate, takes the SPIFFE ID from its SAN, writes the material at `identity/principals/<leaf>` for the leaf of *that* ID, and pairs the key to the certificate by **public-key correspondence** rather than by filename. Reading entry names as paths is what makes archive extraction dangerous, and this format has no path a filename contributed to.

**Entry names still have to be right, as a check rather than a source**: a credential entry's leaf must equal its certificate's, as well as corresponding key's; a `<plane>` segment its envelope's `plane`, and every certificate's trust domain and every envelope's `node` the manifest's. Each disagreement is refused as the producer bug it is. The `node` agreement is the one with an everyday failure behind it — destinations come from `plane` and `name` alone, so a set cut for another node would stage and activate with nothing noticing, leaving that node running another machine's `agent.json`, degraded rather than broken by [ENRL-C0-36](#decision-log)'s rule and therefore silent. There is no entry for the scope's own record: `identity/rete.json` is written from the manifest's `trust_domain` and `node` claims, so each fact has one source rather than two that could disagree. The container's shape, its refusals and its three separable parts are [ENRL-C0-16](#decision-log)'s.

**The bundle carries no fact an artifact already carries.** There is no coordinator URL and no expected coordinator identity: `agent.json`'s `sync` block names the coordinator by principal, and the dial resolves through the agent's own compiler-projected `inbounds` map. And there is no signer list to carry: each envelope in the set brings its own signer's certificate, which a node validates against the anchors **it already holds** — the anchors a bundle delivers are what future artifacts will be checked against, never what checks the bundle itself.

**The first enrollment pins three things, and no later bundle may move any of them** ([ENRL-C0-13](#decision-log)). The **trust domain**, the **anchors** and the **node** are what the scope *is*, and each is a manifest claim under the signature rather than a fact read back out of an entry. A bundle differing from the scope in any of them is refused rather than applied, so re-enrollment reaches principal material and the artifact set alone. The `node` binding is the one with an everyday failure behind it — a bundle carrying node `alpha`'s identity applied to `beta` would silently give `beta` `alpha`'s name — while the other two are what stop an anchor change, the one delivery that would hand a node a new root of trust and the one C0 cannot authorise more strongly than it authorises an ordinary artifact.

**A pin needs both halves, and the second one is `identity/rete.json`** ([ENRL-C0-40](#decision-log)). The anchors are their own file and compare against themselves; the trust domain and the node are claims with nowhere obvious to land, so the record that already held the first now holds the second as well — written by enroll from the manifest, read by whatever needs to know what this scope is. Without it the `node` binding would have been a rule with nothing to check against, since a store of leaf-named credentials cannot say which of them is the node principal.

### What a node can check

**A fresh enrollment verifies no signature, and that is deliberate.** There is nothing on the node to verify against: a signature checked against a key the bundle itself supplies proves nothing, since an attacker who alters a bundle re-signs it and updates the trust material it delivers. What enroll does instead is check every entry against the manifest's digests, then print the trust domain, the anchors' fingerprints and the signer's name, marked plainly as **unverified**. It is a display and not a check: C0 offers no flag turning it into a refusal, because the operator who could supply the expected fingerprints is the operator who cut the bundle, and a second channel in every pilot's critical path buys nothing against the threat it names.

The gain from declining the check is structural: there is then **exactly one signature-verification path in the whole flow**, and it can only be pointed at keys the node already trusts. A path that cannot be handed an attacker-supplied key is worth more than one guarded by care.

**A re-enrollment verifies the manifest signature**, because by then the node holds anchors: it validates the certificate the manifest carries against them and refuses a signer that does not chain. This is not new machinery — it is the same three steps every artifact goes through.

**And it compares before it writes.** The bundle re-delivers the anchors and names the trust domain, so a re-enrollment has both halves of the comparison in hand: the delivered anchor set must equal the pinned one and the delivered trust domain must equal the pinned one, each as **set or string equality**, and a difference in either is a refusal naming which. The comparison is what makes the pin real — without it the anchors would be silently replaceable by anyone who could sign a manifest, which in C0 is anyone holding the mgmt signer key. It also means a bundle cut for the right node against a rete whose anchors have moved fails loudly at the node rather than being discovered later at the first artifact verification.

**Bearer material, honestly.** A bundle carries private keys for its whole life, and **nothing at the bundle level bounds that window** — a control checked by our own tooling binds only the honest party, since an attacker holding a bundle does not run `flor enroll`, they copy PEM files into place. The credential's own validity is the only bound that binds. What remains is guidance — deliver over a channel the recipient trusts, enroll promptly — and one mechanical aid: **`flor enroll` deletes the bundle on success**, or prints a loud instruction where it cannot. That is hygiene rather than a control, and the honest path's leftover copy is the only one this milestone can act on.

## Enrolling a node

One flow serves a first enrollment and every later one — the [Shape](#shape)'s `enroll` block is the sequence, and this section is why each step is where it is rather than a second copy of it. The decision is [ENRL-C0-21](#decision-log).

**A bundle is cut from the source of truth, not from the coordinator** ([ENRL-C0-35](#decision-log)). `retectl issue-bundle` compiles nothing and packs the committed tree, refusing an uncommitted one; that refusal is its whole precondition, so issuing needs no network and asks no coordinator anything. The order is compile, read the diff, commit, publish, issue — and the publish is prompt rather than required, because git is where the rete's state actually lives and the coordinator is a cache of it. A node handed a bundle is not running ahead of the rete. It is current, and the cache is behind.

**What the commit is guarding is a fork, not a delay.** A node's set version is the previous one plus one, counted against the compiled tree, so a tree that rewinds — a checkout, a revert, an abandoned branch, a restore from backup — will reissue a number that a bundle has already given to some node. That node then polls carrying a version the coordinator also holds, receives the empty answer that means *nothing new*, and never converges: it runs a set nobody else has, and both ends read the situation as settled. The coordinator refuses two contents at one version when they arrive through it; a bundle is a second issuance out of the same ledger and never passes that check. Hence the rule worth remembering — **a version handed to any node is spent** — and hence the gate, which cannot make reissuing one impossible but makes it a deliberate, recorded act instead of an accident.

**`retectl publish` stays separate and stays the review gate**, so an operator still reads the diff before nodes see it. What it no longer is is a precondition of anything: gating issuance on it would mean the repair for a dead management node — re-enroll it, which needs a bundle — required the publish that the dead coordinator cannot accept ([ENRL-C0-37](#decision-log)).

**The identity plane is replaced only where the bundle speaks**, because the authoritative statement of which principals live on a node is the **artifact set**, never the bundle. In C0 that difference is small and worth knowing: material for a principal removed from YAML since the last bundle stays on disk, inert, because no peer's access check names it any more.

**The two planes arrive by different channels and at different times, and the gap between them is ordinary** ([ENRL-C0-36](#decision-log)). Publishing precedes issuing, so a running node fetches a set naming a new principal one sync after the change and receives that principal's key and certificate whenever the courier arrives — hours later, or days. That is not a window to be closed but the normal shape of adding a service to a node already in service. What it costs is a principal that is declared, routable and simply not answering: the vertex, which is the component that resolves identity in any case, finds no material for it, opens no listener for it, warns once, and serves every other principal it holds. A caller sees an absent service rather than a handshake that fails, which is the truer report. So the pair of rules is symmetric — **material without an artifact is inert; an artifact without material is degraded** — and neither is an error. What nobody can see is the operator's side: issuance is unrecorded ([ENRL-C0-24](#decision-log)), so `retectl` cannot compare what a published set declares against what has ever been cut, and the gap is visible only in that node's log until C1's ledger exists to close it.

**The artifact set enters through the agent's ordinary intake, not through enroll.** Enroll **deposits** it in `incoming/`; **staging** is a step inside the agent's intake, and nothing outside the agent performs it. The cost of the split is that **enroll succeeds before the set it deposited has been admitted**: its success means the identity plane is written and the files deposited, never that the node is running them, which is why a rejected intake is loud rather than a swept slot.

**Reading `incoming/` before the first dial is what makes one repair possible at all.** A node that has been offline long enough for the coordinator to have moved cannot reach the coordinator to learn where it went — the only thing that could tell it is a set it can no longer fetch. Re-enrollment fixes exactly that, and only because the bundle's set is genuinely admitted rather than assumed stale.

**Enroll takes the scope before it looks at the bundle, and that order is the one thing about the sequence worth memorising** ([ENRL-C0-39](#decision-log)). Every check it performs — which path this run is on, whether the anchors verify the manifest, whether the bundle's claims match what the scope is pinned to — is a fact about the *scope*, not about the bundle, so reading any of them outside the lock is deciding on a value something else may change before the write lands. The natural-reading order, look at what you were handed and then go to the disk, is the wrong one.

**So enroll refuses a live scope rather than quieting it.** It creates `retes/` and the scope root if either is missing, takes the scope's lock, and stops there if someone already holds it — naming the holder, and naming the `flor agent disable` that clears the ordinary one. It does not stop the agent itself: stopping and then acquiring leaves a gap a service manager can restart the agent into, where a refusal leaves none. What that costs is a command, and the person running `enroll` was expecting the scope down for a material change in any case.

**The receipt is what a partial scope root says about itself.** `receipt.json` carries two things, the scope root's `schema_version` and a phase, and enroll writes the phase `enrolling` before any material and replaces it with `enrolled` as its last act. That replacement is the **commit point**: everything before it is a scope that has not been enrolled, which is why an interrupted first enrollment leaves anchors that pin nothing. The phase, not the presence of `anchors.pem`, is what tells a later run whether it is doing a fresh enrollment or a re-enrollment — anchors written by a crashed run would otherwise verify a second bundle against the first bundle's word.

**A failed run therefore converges rather than resuming.** Renaming over a populated directory is atomic on no platform and part of that directory must survive, so atomicity was never available; what stands in for it is that **any bundle this scope would accept completes an interrupted run**. Nothing on disk records which bundle started it, `incoming/` is replaced wholesale rather than merged, and material left by the interrupted run is inert by the same rule that leaves a removed principal's material in place. The operator who re-cut the bundle after a crash is the ordinary case, and it works.

**The scope is the rete's name verbatim.** That name is the SPIFFE trust domain, may be dotted, and **never includes the `.rete` hostname suffix** — `flor.demo` is a scope, `flor.demo.rete` is not; the suffix belongs to hostnames of the form `<service>.<rete>.rete`. The only constraint is length, since the scope appears in the agent's socket path and macOS bounds those. Two independent retes that chose the same name cannot both be enrolled on one host, and that is a refusal naming the conflict rather than a flag to work around.

**`retes/` is created by whichever of the installer and enroll runs first**, identically, through one library function ([ENRL-C0-22](#decision-log)) — creating the directory is not writing *into* it, and a distribution package places binaries without ever invoking `flor install`, so enroll cannot assume it exists. Modes and ownership below it are [Agent § Node layout](../agent#node-layout)'s.

### Without an operator present

A server enrolled over SSH, a provisioning script, and a machine enrolling at first boot are **the same path**: nothing about writing an identity store changes when nobody is watching, and every check enroll performs is mechanical and runs identically either way ([ENRL-C0-23](#decision-log)).

**No flag makes an image carry an identity.** Enrolling during an image build gives every instance from that image one node identity, and baking a *bundle* into the image does not fix it — a bundle is per node too, so an image carrying one enrolls every instance as the same node. An image therefore carries **no identity at all**, and each instance receives its own bundle at first boot through some channel outside the image: a provisioning system, an operator over SSH, or eventually a credential fetched by the node itself. Building one image per node is the other answer, and it is a fleet answer only where the fleet is small.

## Activation

**Enroll activates nothing** ([ENRL-C0-20](#decision-log)). `flor agent enable` is the only thing that does, and enrolling a node is therefore two commands rather than one command with two steps: enroll writes the material, prints the `enable` that finishes the job, and exits — releasing the scope lock as it goes, which is the point. Enroll cannot both hold that lock and start the party that needs it, and releasing early would put its last act outside the lock it took to make the act safe; handing activation to `enable` removes the choice instead of picking the less bad half.

**What that costs is a node that stays down until someone enables it**, where enroll used to bring it back by itself. The mitigation is a printed command rather than a mechanism, which is the same answer `flor install` gives when it reports each surviving scope's `enable` line and starts nothing. A node that is enrolled-but-inactive is a resting state this design already relies on: it is exactly what survives an uninstall, resumable with no bundle at all — the alternative charges a re-issued bundle for a reinstall, and a bundle is bearer material that must be couriered. It also lets a multi-rete node leave one rete without touching the others. `disable` is neither revocation nor purge, and it is what an operator runs to hand a live scope over to an enroll.

**"Activate" names an act, never a service manager.** Today it instantiates the wrapper the installer placed — see [Installer § Service registration](../../installer#service-registration) — but a nested rete, where one rete's agent runs as a supervised workload of another's, would make activation *adding a supervision entry* instead. The installer owns the wrapper and this page owns the act, so nothing forecloses that; the contract simply does not name systemd.

## Revocation and rotation

**C0 does not have a revocation mechanism. It has a name change**, and saying so plainly is more useful than the mechanism sounding larger than it is.

The operator removes the principal from `users.yaml` or `services.yaml`, compiles, and publishes; every node drops it from its ingress tables at the next sync. A still-live private key opens nothing, because no peer's access check names that principal any more. What this costs is the **name**: re-admitting the same person or service means a new entry, and every ACL, group and role that referenced the old one has to be rewritten ([ENRL-C0-24](#decision-log)).

**"At the next sync" is the whole of the guarantee, and it is per node rather than rete-wide.** A revocation takes effect at each peer when that peer converges, so between the publish and the last node's sync the principal is refused by some and admitted by others — effective access during a transition being the union across nodes that have not caught up ([ENRL-C0-38](#decision-log)). For a coordinator-fed node that is a poll interval. For a node fed mainly by courier it is however long until it next syncs, which nothing in C0 shortens and no mechanism enforces. Revocation is therefore eventual, and its deadline is the slowest channel that reaches the node you care about.

**Git records a principal's declaration and removal, and nothing else.** It does not record **issuance**: `retectl issue-bundle` mints private keys and signs certificates without committing anything, the declaration may precede issuance by any interval, and re-issuance leaves no trace. So the revocation record is genuinely the commit, and the enrollment record does not exist in this milestone. The operator-side issuance ledger that would supply it arrives with C1's enrollment log.

**Rotation has one path and it is re-enrollment**: a fresh bundle, couriered, replacing every principal on the node at once. Policy is therefore rete-wide, because **policy granularity may not exceed mechanism granularity** ([ENRL-C0-25](#decision-log)). The ninety-day default is a placeholder with a stated reason rather than a considered value.

**The criterion for a real number is unusual here and belongs on the page.** A credential's lifetime must exceed the longest period its holder may be unable to reach an issuer. Hour-long certificates are free for users on corporate networks and wrong for a device out of contact for a week, which would brick itself for reasons unrelated to any attack. **And a companion bounds it from above: no certificate may outlive the root that issued it** — a leaf minted late in the root's life would fail at the *root's* expiry while still looking valid, fleet-wide and after the material is couriered. Every mint therefore **clamps** `notAfter` to the root's and warns, so late in a root's life the fleet's credentials shorten rather than issuance stopping dead.

**Expiry is surfaced by whatever touches a certificate** ([ENRL-C0-33](#decision-log)): enroll on the anchors and every principal's certificate as it writes them, the agent on the anchors and each signer it admits, the vertex on its own leaf, `retectl` on the root and the signer. Two of those fire with a human present, which is the whole of their value. **None of it binds anything**: a log line can be missed, and C0's actual mechanism is the operator's calendar against a known ninety days.

**Rotation defends against material an attacker can no longer renew — and the distinguishing property is presence, not volume.** A credential that leaked into an image, a chat log or a backup stops working; so does everything taken by an intruder who exfiltrated a node whole and then left, which is what a malicious dependency or a supply-chain compromise looks like and is the case that actually happens. What no rotation scheme addresses is an attacker **still resident**, who rotates alongside you. Read every rotation claim on this page against that line.

### Signer trust

The mgmt signer is what every artifact's signature is checked against, and its trust has an anchor rather than only a chain: every envelope carries its signer's certificate, the agent validates it against the node's anchors and then checks the signature, and no node persists a signer set to be out of date about ([ENRL-C0-12](#decision-log)). Rotation is minting a fresh certificate from the root CA and signing with it — no add-switch-retire, no overlap window, and a node that was switched off while it happened accepts the successor on the anchor's word when it returns.

**Anchoring it in the root CA is what makes a leaked signer survivable.** A pure vouched-successor chain over bare public keys has no anchor and no escape: an attacker holding the current signer's private key could introduce their own and retire the operator's, and the operator's cold root CA could not overrule it. Requiring a CA signature closes that with material the node already holds — a self-minted key is never CA-vouched, so it cannot enter the set however it is delivered. The stolen key still signs artifacts until its certificate expires; what it loses is the ability to introduce a successor, and — because the anchors are pinned — the ability to hand a node a new root of trust through a re-enrollment bundle.

**Losing a signer key and leaking one are different events with different remedies, and only the first has one.** A **lost** key is recovered from the root: mint a fresh certificate, replace the operator's pair, sign with it, and every node accepts on the anchor's word — no re-enrollment, no visit. A **leaked** key has no remedy in C0 at all. There is no signer list to edit, revocation is deferred milestone-wide, and the pin means re-enrollment cannot help either: it re-delivers the same anchors, which go on vouching for the leaked certificate until it expires. So the blast radius of a leaked mgmt signer is `signers.mgmt.validity_days` — a year, by default — or a new rete, which is the same bound a leaked root has. The two keys sharing a directory is what makes that equivalence honest rather than alarming: a leak reaching one has reached the other.

**`identity/anchors.pem` is a set, not a certificate — and in C0 a set that never changes.** It arrives once at the first enrollment and is immutable afterwards; the plural shape is what a rotation would need, reserved without being built. **Rotating the root CA is not something C0 does**, and the three ways it comes up — planned, lost key, compromised key — share no mechanism and all end in the same place: build a new rete, remove every scope root by hand, enroll fresh. That is also why the pin costs so little, since the mechanism a graceful rotation would ride does not exist in any case. [ENRL-C0-13](#decision-log) has the three cases and what a successor would owe.

**The recursion stops at the root CA, and that is the irreducible root.** Signers are anchored by it, so a *lost* signer is recoverable and a leaked one is at least bounded by its certificate's expiry. Nothing anchors the root CA: a compromised root key can cross-sign its own successor and lock the operator out. That is not a second instance of the same defect — below the root CA there was a colder authority available and unused, and above it there is none. An attacker holding the root key already mints any principal at will, so the additional loss is nil, and the recovery is re-enrolling every node by hand.

## Evolution sketches

Recorded so nothing above forecloses them. None is designed and none is a commitment; each names the property this milestone owes it, and each cites the decision that defers it.

- **A credential spent at first use.** *([ENRL-C0-27](#decision-log))* The operator mints two credentials offline; the bundle carries only the first, and the second — **encrypted to the node's current public key** — is relayed by the coordinator as opaque bytes and deleted on fetch, so a second attempt fails visibly. This needs no online signing authority, gives an attacker-first race a *detectable* outcome, and is what replaces the unbounded bearer window with consumption. It states a rule this design was missing: an artifact needs integrity and authenticity, which a signature gives it; a credential needs **confidentiality** as well, because the coordinator sits in the path and is not a party.
- **Replacing a credential over the mgmt channel.** *([ENRL-C0-27](#decision-log))* Re-enrollment is the **least secure delivery path this design has** and is currently the only rotation path: the mgmt channel is mutually authenticated and ours, while the courier channel is a chat app and a stick in a pocket. What differs between the successors is who *produced* the material, not how it travels. The property owed: a principal's certificate and key must be replaceable **on their own**, without touching the artifact set or any other principal's material.
- **Bounding a hot issuer by chaining credentials.** *([ENRL-C0-28](#decision-log))* When issuance is automated, a new credential's public key is signed by its **predecessor's private key**, which the node holds and a ladder-bound issuer never does — so a hijacked issuer can mint a certificate carrying no continuity proof, and peers that have met that principal reject it. The issuer stops being something that must be *trusted* and becomes something that must merely be *available*. Continuity is opportunistic: a peer with no history falls back to the anchors, so coverage grows with the age of the rete.
- **Rotating what C0 pins.** *([ENRL-C0-13](#decision-log))* The anchors and the trust domain are fixed at the first enrollment, so a rete that must change either is replaced. A successor would need three things this milestone does not build: an **add** path, which a cross-signed successor riding the mgmt plane could give; a **remove** path, which nothing can carry over a channel the retiring anchor authenticates, so it is out of band or it is nowhere; and an **authorisation** for an anchor change that is strictly stronger than a mgmt signature, since a signer that can rewrite the anchors is a signer that can take a node. The plural `anchors.pem` is the one piece already in place, and it is shape rather than mechanism. A **spare anchor seeded at genesis** looks like the cheap half of the same answer and owes none of the three, yet is [rejected](#decision-log) rather than deferred — the reasons all turn on the missing remove path, and it becomes a real option only once one exists. Changing the trust domain is a different question again and probably has no answer: it is the rete's name and every principal's SPIFFE ID is scoped by it.
- **A set identified by its content rather than its position.** *([ENRL-C0-35](#decision-log))* The commit gate makes reissuing a spent version deliberate rather than accidental; it cannot make it impossible, because a version is an ordinal and two different contents can wear the same one. Carrying the set's **digest** alongside its version in the node's high-water-mark would close that: a node holding v9 of one content, meeting a coordinator serving v9 of another, would detect the fork where today it reads an empty answer as *nothing new*. The mark is small and additive, and `agent.json` already has a door left open for a per-member digest map. What makes it a design rather than a field is everything it opens behind it — a set identity that is a content hash, a predecessor it can name, a chain of them that is a lineage — which is git's construction arriving by a second route, and not by coincidence: both are versioning a tree of files that must be recognised across parties who cannot talk. Whether to lean on git for it, to mirror its mechanisms, or to build something of our own is the question, and it deserves its own pass rather than a field added here.
- **An encrypted bundle.** *([ENRL-C0-29](#decision-log))* Encrypting only the secret entries keeps the container readable, so the manifest, the trust domain and the fingerprints are verified and displayed *before* a recipient is asked for a secret — and a re-enrollment needs no secret at all, the node's existing public key being the recipient key.
- **An intermediate, so the root can genuinely stay offline.** *([ENRL-C0-06](#decision-log))* The recommendation to move the root out is undercut by the root being read on every `issue-bundle`, which is a standing reason not to move it. The answer PKI has always used is a root that signs an **intermediate**, an intermediate that does day-to-day issuance, and a root touched only to rotate that intermediate. It is not C0's: every verifier would have to build and validate a path rather than check a signature, and the signer check would follow. It is what makes the offline root practical rather than aspirational.
- **Delivery that is not a bundle.** *([ENRL-C0-29](#decision-log))* Nothing may assume the bundle format is the only way a credential reaches a node; a courier file a human handles once and a machine-to-machine payload over mTLS need not share a format.
- **A separable anchor and a replaceable issuer.** *([ENRL-C0-30](#decision-log))* Exactly one thing is common to a fleet — the anchors — so a two-phase provisioning split moves one field. And the rete CA is one implementation of a boundary rather than a fixture: **the identity store is a sink, and nothing may assume what filled it.** An identity may one day be created by a component this project does not ship.
- **Naming the coordinator rather than its address.** *([ENRL-C0-31](#decision-log))* From the milestone where a node joins at a link to *some* node, its position is not known when its material is issued. `sync` names a principal, and nothing may carry the coordinator's address as the primary fact.
- **Foreign and temporary principals.** *([ENRL-C0-32](#decision-log))* An `ingress.allow` entry names a full SPIFFE ID and therefore already expresses a **foreign** principal; an entry must never be shortened to a bare local name. A **temporary** grant is a short-lived identity rather than a time-boxed permission, because the latter needs validity windows in the artifact *and* clock agreement between every enforcing pair.

## Decision Log

Entries are grouped by what a future change would be touching, not by the order they were settled. Ten of them are deliberate deferrals and each names the property C0 owes it.

### Tooling and command surface

**ENRL-C0-01. Split the CLI by the state a command authors.**
`retectl` authors rete state; `flor` authors node state. A private key is *material* rather than state — it has a holder and no author, and where it sits decides nothing. Minting therefore belongs to `retectl`, because a SPIFFE name enters the rete's YAML and the certificate is CA-vouched, both facts the rete holds. `retectl` reaching no node state is an **invariant it keeps**, not a limitation it has.
*Because* the criterion has to classify a command nobody has written yet, and only this one does. Audience partitions people, and people overlap — the operator's workstation is itself an enrolled node. Scope-of-effect cannot classify minting, its own motivating case. A closed list of `flor`'s commands fails on the first unanticipated one: a node-local override is not install, uninstall, enroll, agent or version, so a list would route it to `retectl`, the one tool that must never touch it.
*Accepting* that the boundary is argued per command rather than read off a list, and that the state-versus-material distinction has to be taught before it can be applied.
*Rejected* audience, on the overlap above; scope of effect, superseded by this reading of it; node-maintenance-versus-everything, on the override case; authoring-versus-running, which has no cell for `flor install`.

**ENRL-C0-02. Name a `retectl` command group for its subject, never its actor.**
A bare command is a verb whose implicit subject is the rete — `compile`, `publish`, `issue-bundle`. Where a group exists, the group's name is the subject its verbs act on: `ca init` creates the authority, `id issue` produces an identity. A command that emits a *principal's* certificate belongs to `id`, whatever instrument signs it.
*Because* the scar exists: `ca sign` was named for the actor, read as *the authority mints*, and written into this page as a mint — contradicting the CLI reference and the implementation for long enough to need a ticket.
*Accepting* that the rule bites only when a group is introduced, which is rare — and that its value is concentrated exactly there, since a group name is what a reader uses to guess what a command does before running it.

**ENRL-C0-03. Give the identity plane four commands and one front door.**
`retectl init <name>` creates the repo scaffold, the root CA and the `management-plane/primary` signer, and is the only way a root CA comes into existence. `retectl id issue` produces a key that **stays in the operator's custody** — that is its subject, and accepting signing kinds only is the consequence, those being the principals whose keys must never leave. `retectl issue-bundle` mints and packs every TLS principal on one node. `id keygen` and `id sign` are named for Flow B and are not part of C0.
*Because* a key that must never leave operator hardware is **issued**, and a key that must reach a node is **bundled** — which is this design's existing split between signing-principal and TLS-principal material, expressed as a command surface rather than invented. A TLS key issued directly would have nowhere to go — bundles are per node, so no per-principal delivery exists to reach for — and the definition survives rotation, which issues TLS credentials from machinery rather than from this command. Genesis earns a front door because it happens once per rete at the worst moment for a new operator, while rotation forces the primitives to exist anyway.
*Accepting* that C0 has no `ca` group at all; it returns when CA rotation lands, and ENRL-C0-02 already says where its commands go.
*Revisit* the Flow B carve-out when Flow B is designed: the grouping is settled, the two commands' argument shapes are not.
*Revisit* the custody clause when a **ctrl** signer stops being operator-held. `id issue` accepts both signing kinds and describes both as staying in the operator's custody, which is true while a `control-plane` key is minted for an operator-run signer — C0 and C1 — and stops being true at B1, where [ADR-0015](/docs/implementation/adr/0015-anchor-signer-trust-in-root-ca) makes the ctrl signer a running service, hot by design and hosted rather than held. That key then needs delivery, which is a bundle's job and not this command's, so what the milestone owes is a decision about *which* command mints a hosted signer — not a wider `id issue`.
*Rejected* folding the root CA into `id`, since a CA is not a principal; a mint-or-sign command whose behaviour depends on whether an argument was supplied, which is the contradiction that prompted this; composing genesis by hand, which writes a freshly-minted private key to an intermediate file.

**ENRL-C0-04. Put a node-local override in `flor`, outside the signed set.**
Overriding an inbound or outbound locally authors node state, so it is `flor`'s. The command and the merge mechanics are [flor#44](https://github.com/rete-labs/flor/issues/44)'s; two properties are owed to it. An override must live **outside** `artifacts/`, because sets are immutable, staged by version and swept by the cleanup pass, so anything inside is either destroyed at the next sync or indistinguishable from tampering. And an override may change only **what this node does** — anything that changes what another node does is a rete fact, since reachability needs other nodes' ingress and route entries, which only the compiler produces and only the coordinator distributes.
*Because* a legitimate override has to be distinguishable from tampering, and under the documented hand-edit posture the two are the same act.
*Accepting* that "add a new inbound or outbound locally" collapses in both directions: an inbound nobody can route to is not a published service, and an outbound needs peer routing the rete had to supply anyway. Override yes; add no.

**ENRL-C0-05.** *Superseded by [ENRL-C0-35](#decision-log).*

**ENRL-C0-35. Pack only what is committed, and let the coordinator lag.**
The operator sequence is **compile → commit → publish → issue-bundle → enroll**, and `retectl issue-bundle` compiles nothing: it packs the node's set out of the compiled tree and **refuses when that tree is not committed**, naming the commit. That is its whole precondition — it consults no coordinator, needs no network, and has no special case for a rete that has never published. `retectl publish` stays a separate command and stays the review gate, the diff being read between compile and commit; it is **prompt rather than required**, since publishing is what stops the cache lagging the source.
*Because* **git is the rete's source of truth and the coordinator is a cache of it.** A node fed from a bundle is therefore not *ahead* of anything — it is current, and the coordinator is stale. Read the other way round the gate lands on the cache, which is both the wrong authority and, per [ENRL-C0-37](#decision-log), a dependency the bundle path must not have.
*Because* the hazard the ordering exists for is **a fork, not a delay**. A version is `previous + 1` against the compiled tree, so a tree that rewinds — a checkout, a revert, an abandoned branch, a restore — reissues a number some bundle already handed to a node. That node polls with a version the coordinator also holds, gets the empty answer that means *nothing new*, and **never converges**: it runs a set no one else has, and both ends read it as steady state. The coordinator's `409` refuses equal-version-different-bytes on the path through it; a bundle is a second issuance out of the same ledger and routes around that check. So the invariant is **a version handed to any node is spent**, and the commit is what makes reissuing one a deliberate, recorded act rather than an accident.
*Because* **the check must be offline to be worth anything**: it guards the channel whose reason for existing is that the other one is unavailable. The compiled tree's own commit state answers it with no receipt to maintain, no ref to move and nothing to ask the network.
*Because* it makes true a claim the design already makes. The git log is described as the rete's shipment history, and nothing currently requires that what shipped was ever committed.
*Because* the repair path of [ENRL-C0-21](#decision-log) survives and is sharper: a node offline long enough for the coordinator to have moved is repaired from the source of truth directly, which is the one place that cannot itself be unreachable.
*Accepting* that **`issue-bundle` acquires a hard dependency on git**, where [ENRL-C0-08](#decision-log) deliberately anchored discovery on `rete.yaml` rather than on `.git`. Git stops being convention here and becomes load-bearing. It is payable because the repository is a git repository in every other part of this design, and it is the pick's real cost rather than a detail.
*Accepting* that **the commit gate narrows the fork without closing it**: an operator who rewinds the committed tree and compiles forward can still reissue a spent version. Closing it needs a set's identity to be its content rather than its position, which is a design of its own and is deferred — see [Evolution sketches](#evolution-sketches).
*Accepting* that **the artifact set now reaches a running node before the credentials it names**, for the courier's whole duration and on every change that adds a principal. Under the superseded entry the two travelled together. This is the standard sequence rather than an anomaly, and [ENRL-C0-36](#decision-log) is what pays for it.
*Accepting* that genesis gains an explicit compile and commit, once per rete.
*Revisit* at the publish design, which owes what publish requires of the repository, whether the review gate acquires a mechanism, how publish serialises once a rete has more than one operator machine, and whether the coordinator should report a node polling above what it holds — worth more than it looked, since what that detects is a fork rather than a lag.
*Rejected* **compiling inside `issue-bundle`**, the superseded [ENRL-C0-05](#decision-log): it kept the packed set equal to what *would* be published and said nothing about what *was*, so it minted a spent version while appearing to prevent one, and charged a whole-rete compile per bundle for the appearance. Its own rejection of "refusing on a stale tree" does not carry over — that was a staleness test against YAML, which the compiler cannot perform; this is a test of whether the tree is committed.
*Rejected* **gating on a successful publish**, which reads well and is a cycle: a dead management node is repaired by re-enrolling it, re-enrolling needs a bundle, and that bundle would need the publish the dead coordinator cannot accept. It also needs a record of published state that a bundle-cutting operator may be offline to consult, and it invents a genesis carve-out to excuse the one case where the cycle is unavoidable.
*Rejected* folding publish into `issue-bundle`, which cannot run at genesis and would make the one offline command networked; publish also packing pending bundles, which emits bearer material as a side effect for nodes nobody meant to re-key; packing the last published set, which fails on the main case, a newly declared node being absent from it; compiling only the packed node, which produces a set differing from what will be published; and a warning rather than a refusal, which loses on timing — the output is couriered for days, so the moment of the warning and the moment of the harm are unrelated.

### Operator-held material

**ENRL-C0-06. Keep the rete's root CA and signer in `retectl`'s own tree.**
`~/.retectl`, overridden whole by `RETECTL_HOME`, holds one identity store per rete at `retes/<scope>/identity/` — `authorities/root.crt|.key` for the root CA, `principals/<name>.crt|.key` for the signers — at `0700` with every key at `0600`, plus everything else `retectl` owns, its configuration included. The root key stays there in C0: no command takes a path to it.
*Because* one tree is what this material needs: its defining operation is being moved as a unit, backed up, encrypted, or carried to another host, and the XDG bases exist to scatter an application across four of them. Every comparable credential-holding tool — ssh, gpg, step, pass, kubectl, docker — keeps one tree for the same reason.
*Because* the store shape is the node's: the categories are the identity model's own rather than either side's — a holder has anchors, the authorities it holds and the principals it holds — and each side populates what it has, a node's `authorities/` being empty in C0 and an operator's `anchors.pem` being. `authorities/` rather than `ca/` follows the repository's rule that a directory of named instances takes the plural collection name.
*Accepting* that the root key sits in a predictable path under the operator's home — step-ca's trade, taken deliberately. Moving it offline is **not** recommended in C0: nothing could name the moved key, and an offline root read on every `issue-bundle` is a recommendation nobody would follow. Both belong with the intermediate that makes them practical.
*Revisit* when an intermediate or a CA service lands and the root key stops being read on every issuance.
*Rejected* the node's scope root, which `retectl` may not touch and which re-enrollment replaces wholesale; the rete repo, on ENRL-C0-07; no default at all, which forces the operator to decide where a root of trust lives before they have a rete; `XDG_STATE_HOME`, which the specification defines as what is *not important or portable enough* for `XDG_DATA_HOME` — the exact inverse of this material.

**ENRL-C0-07. Keep every secret and every certificate out of the rete repo.**
The repo carries YAML source and the compiled tree, both committed deliberately so the git log is a shipment history. It carries no private key, and no public certificate either: `rete.yaml` names a signer, and the certificate resolves in `retectl`'s tree. Bundles go to a gitignored `<repo>/bundles/<node>.bundle`, always and with no override, and that directory is transient by design.
*Because* a repo's defining operation is being **copied** — cloned to a second workstation, checked out in CI, archived by a backup — so a private key inside one is wrong whatever git tracks. And the audit case for committing certificates does not survive checking: every compiled envelope carries the certificate of the signer that signed it, and the compiled tree is already committed, so a separate copy adds no fact a reviewer did not have. Bundles are the exception because they are not durable: the repo is where the operator already stands to compile and publish, and a default of "wherever you were standing" scatters bearer files nobody can later find.
*Accepting* that the repo no longer shows the trust anchor in a human-readable place, and that a reviewer reads it out of a compiled artifact; and that a spent bundle survives in the workspace until deleted, since nothing can know it was consumed.
*Rejected* a durable bundle directory in `retectl`'s tree, which would accumulate spent bundles still carrying live keys; a temporary directory removed on exit, which forces delivery to happen inside the issuing command.

**ENRL-C0-08. Require a repo root, take the scope from it, and let no command name a path.**
`retectl` walks up from the working directory to find `rete.yaml`, as git finds `.git`, and refuses to run outside one; `retectl init` inverts the same test, refusing inside one. The scope is the **rete name verbatim** — the SPIFFE trust domain, possibly dotted, never including the `.rete` hostname TLD — and a collision between two independent retes of that name is a refusal naming the conflict. **No command takes a path**: no `--repo`, no `--out`, no `-f`. `compile` always writes `<repo>/.flor/compiled/` and `issue-bundle` always `<repo>/bundles/<node>.bundle`; narrowing the source set is `source.include`/`source.exclude` in `rete.yaml`.
*Because* the structure is the point: paths become repo-relative, bundles land in one place per rete, and the scope comes for free, which removes a `--rete` flag from every command that would have needed one. `rete.yaml` is already the anchor, so a marker directory would be a second one for the same job — and a renamed `rete.name` is a different trust domain rather than an edited convenience, so the tree moving with it is correct.
*Because* each rejected flag reintroduces the ambiguity the repo root removes. A named root and a discovered root are two discovery rules, and the flag version is the one that lets a command run against a repository the operator is not standing in. `-f` is worse: it selects files with no root to anchor them, so requiring that some selected file carry the `rete` block is the rule admitting it has lost the anchor. And an output override is wrong on a stronger ground than consistency — **the compiled tree is an input to the next compile**, being the version ledger [ENRL-C0-35](#decision-log) relies on, so a tree written elsewhere compiles from the wrong history and produces versions the coordinator rejects or, worse, accepts.
*Accepting* that CI runs the same command in the same layout as an operator, which is the point rather than a limitation; that a partial or scratch compile has no expression in C0 and gets one by copying the repo; and that a rete repo becomes required rather than recommended.
*Revisit* `--as <scope>` when a host actually joins two same-named retes; adding it later is additive, and a flag nobody exercises is a flag nobody has tested.

**ENRL-C0-09. Share flor's host configuration, none of its state, and exactly one of its locations.**
`retectl` reads no flor configuration; its log stream defaults to stderr and to off, and `RUST_LOG` turns it on. It resolves one flor path — `agent.sock` — because `publish` reaches `coordinator-publisher` through the local agent's SOCKS5 listener. Per rete it is told exactly one thing, the **operator's own principal name**, and derives the rest: the SOCKS5 address through `agent.sock`, the publish target from the reserved service name plus `rete.name`. `retectl init` writes `~/.retectl/retes/<scope>/config.toml` as a commented template; `publish` with the key unset fails naming the file, the key and the principals holding the `operator` role. [CLI § retectl local wiring](../cli#retectl-local-wiring) has the mechanics.
*Because* the log stream exists for processes nobody is watching, and `retectl` changes rete state — whose record is the git commit — with the person who ran it watching the terminal.
*Because* configure-the-stable, derive-the-volatile: a principal name changes only by a deliberate rete-level act, while a SOCKS5 address is compiler-projected and goes stale silently. Resolving the operator from the repo cannot work at all — the repo describes the rete, and the missing fact is which human is at the keyboard.
*Because* **the publish target is the opposite case and is therefore derived**: `coordinator-publisher` and its group are reserved names the validator enforces ([Coordinator](../coordinator)), so the target is fixed by the rete rather than chosen per operator — and `retectl` is the compiler, which already resolves that principal to fill every node's `agent.json` sync block.
*Because* **the template is written rather than prompted for**: the value names a human, so it travels by no channel the rete has. It cannot be committed, does not follow a `git clone` — `~/.retectl` being outside the repository — and must not be copied even alongside trust material, the receiving operator being a different principal. There is no delivery to design, only a place to write it and a failure that teaches.
*Accepting* that `flor`'s commands log to syslog and `retectl`'s do not, so the uniform output rule covers stdout-for-data and stderr-for-people but not the log stream; and that `--socks5` must be settable in configuration and not only as a flag, since C0 may ship without `agent.sock` at all, in which case configure-the-address is the accepted degradation.
*Revisit* when `agent.sock` ships: the configured address becomes the fallback and the principal the normal path.
*Rejected* a coordinator URL in `rete.yaml`, which duplicates a reserved name into authored source; configuring the target beside the principal, which fails this entry's own criterion — the target is determined by context where only the typist is not; `--target` per invocation, an argument that never varies; and an interactive prompt at `init`, against [ENRL-C0-23](#decision-log)'s unattended posture.

**ENRL-C0-10. Keep `agent.sock` internal to flor's own tools.**
It serves `flor agent status`, `flor agent sync`, and `retectl`'s operator lookup, and nothing else in C0. It carries no version.
*Because* `retectl` and `flor` ship as one release and are upgraded together, so on a single host the two ends of this socket cannot skew — the property one release version over four executables was bought for. A third party has no such guarantee, so inviting one means versioning the protocol, which should wait for a consumer to design it for.
*Rejected* public and versioned, as premature; public and unversioned, which is a dependency people would take and nobody priced.

### Trust model

**ENRL-C0-34. Support exactly one operator machine, and say so.**
The rete's root key, its mgmt signer key and `retectl`'s per-rete `config.toml` all live in `~/.retectl` and travel by no channel C0 builds: a `git clone` brings the repository and none of them. C0 therefore has **one operator machine** — the one that ran `retectl init` — and no command moves, copies or re-creates what it holds.
*Because* the three pieces do not decompose the way "support a second operator" suggests. **The root key and a signer key are separable and would not travel together**: a root can stay with one operator while a signer is copied to a second, which is a real and probably desirable arrangement, and designing the copy before anyone needs it would settle that split by guesswork. `config.toml` must never be copied at all, naming as it does a particular human. A single "move my rete" operation would be wrong in one of its three parts and premature in the other two.
*Accepting* that losing the operator's machine loses the rete — the same exposure [ENRL-C0-06](#decision-log) already takes by keeping the root key at a predictable path with no passphrase. **The remedy is a backup of `~/.retectl`, and it is C0's only remedy for a lost root key**, the spare anchor having been rejected in [ENRL-C0-13](#decision-log) partly on this ground. The mechanism is the operator's own — any file backup restores the tree, since it is one directory by construction — but the *instruction* is ours to write, and it belongs in the operator guide, [#14](https://github.com/rete-labs/florete/issues/14).
*Revisit* with multi-operator, which owes four separate answers rather than one: how a **signer** is copied without the root, how a **root** is moved rather than copied, where per-operator configuration comes from on a machine that never ran `init`, and how **publish serialises** once two machines can run it. The likely shape of the third is splitting `retectl init`, which today conflates *make a repository* with *mint a rete* in a way `git init` does not. The fourth is the one with a mechanism already in sight and it is not the coordinator's: the source of truth is git, the coordinator holds output and no ancestry, and its monotonic `409` rejects a stale publish without being able to tell a stale base from a legitimate one. It belongs to the publish design of [ENRL-C0-35](#decision-log), where "nobody published behind your back" can reduce to "nobody pushed behind your back".
*Rejected* an export/import pair now, on the guesswork above; splitting `init` now, which buys nothing until a second machine exists to run the second half.

**ENRL-C0-11. Vouch for principals with a certificate authority, not a public-key registry.**
One CA per rete signs every principal's certificate; a peer validates a presented certificate against the anchor and needs nothing per-peer.
*Because* a CA lets a peer accept a caller **it has never heard of**, which is what makes trust-domain federation expressible at all. A registry cannot: someone must have pushed that caller's public key first, so the caller set is closed by construction, and a public inter-rete service — open by definition — cannot be expressed. The cost argument compounds it: a registry makes every node hold every permitted peer's key, and inter-rete that becomes the product of two retes' principal sets, so the partial per-node projections this design is committed to are unreachable, a partial registry being a peer you cannot authenticate.
*Accepting* a key that must be protected, and the whole rotation burden that follows from it.
*Rejected* a registry, on the federation argument — noting that both mesh-VPN comparables use one, correctly, for populations that are enumerable.

### Bundle shape and verification

**ENRL-C0-12. Carry the signer's certificate with what it signed, and validate it against the anchors.**
Every envelope carries its signer's certificate inside its own `signature`, which becomes `{ cert, value }`; `key_id` is gone, the certificate carrying the SPIFFE ID in its SAN, and `alg` with it, the key type fixing the scheme so that no signed document selects the routine that checks it. A bundle's manifest carries it the same way. Verification is: **extract the certificate, validate it against the trust anchors this node holds, check that its kind matches the plane, check the signature** — in that order. The kind is the SAN's first path segment — `management-plane` for a mgmt artifact and for a bundle manifest, `control-plane` for a ctrl one — and it is **load-bearing rather than tidy**: no X.509 extension separates a signing leaf from a TLS one, so without it every leaf the root ever signed is a mgmt signer, a user's included. No mgmt artifact carries a signer set, no node persists one, and **C0 narrows mgmt signers no further**: the kind is the whole of the constraint. Rotating a signer is minting a fresh certificate from the root CA, replacing the operator's pair, and signing with it. [ADR-0015](/docs/implementation/adr/0015-anchor-signer-trust-in-root-ca) is the record.
*Because* this is the standard flow — CMS `SignedData`, JWS `x5c`, every code-signing scheme — and the pinned set it replaces was a bespoke layer whose two defects were costs of the layer rather than of the problem. Over bare public keys the chain had **no anchor**: an attacker with the current signer's key could introduce their own and retire the operator's, and the cold root could not overrule it. And the chain required seeing **every step**, which a node absent across a rotation never does, the coordinator serving only the latest tree — so absence was fatal and an overlap window could only postpone it.
*Because* an envelope must be verifiable **alone**: the agent verifies each artifact independently, the coordinator re-serves each byte-for-byte, and ctrl artifacts are independent streams with no set root to hang a signer list from.
*Because* self-carriage is not the circularity the superseded record forbade. That prohibition was against an artifact **authorising** its own signer; here it merely **presents** one, and the authority to accept it is external and predates it, having arrived at enrollment.
*Because* **a plane may constrain the plane below it, never itself** — which is why the ctrl list survives and a mgmt one cannot. `agent.json` is a mgmt artifact carrying an ACL over ctrl, so it never gates its own delivery; a mgmt list inside a mgmt artifact would be verified against the list it is updating, which is the deadlock. Mgmt is not therefore unconstrained: **the anchors are its ACL**, arriving out of band and delivered by no plane at all. The hierarchy is anchors → mgmt → ctrl.
*Accepting* that **C0 cannot block a leaked mgmt signer**, and that losing a signer key and leaking one are not the same event. A **lost** key is recovered from the root — mint a fresh certificate, replace the pair, sign with it, and every node accepts on the anchor's word, including one that was switched off. A **leaked** key has no remedy at all: there is no list to edit, revocation is deferred milestone-wide, and re-enrollment cannot displace it, since [ENRL-C0-13](#decision-log) pins the anchors that go on vouching for it. Its bound is `signers.mgmt.validity_days` or a new rete — the bound a leaked root has. The equivalence is honest rather than alarming: the two keys share a directory, so a leak reaching one has reached the other.
*Because* **the block names the signer and nothing about the checking**, which is why it carries no `alg` either. The certificate's key type fixes the verification scheme, and the verifier reaches that certificate only after validating it to the anchors — so the scheme is derived from something already trusted rather than read out of the document being checked, which is the shape behind the JWS algorithm-confusion class. It also matches the digest rule of [ENRL-C0-16](#decision-log), which fixes SHA-256 with no algorithm field; one block carrying an algorithm and its neighbour omitting one would have been an inconsistency with no argument behind it.
*Accepting* a certificate per envelope: a few hundred bytes against a handful of artifacts per node.
*Accepting* that **adding a key type means fixing its signature scheme with it**, as a rete-wide policy act rather than a per-envelope field — cheap, since the schemes worth adopting are self-determining: `id-Ed25519` names key and signature together, and the ML-DSA and SLH-DSA parameter sets do the same. ECDSA underdetermines only its hash, pinned by curve in every modern profile, and unrestricted RSA is the sole genuinely ambiguous case.
*Revisit* only if a key type ever arrives with two admissible schemes. The field returns then as an ordinary [ADR-0012](/docs/implementation/adr/0012-version-artifact-contract) minor bump — minimum-minor stamping keeps unupgraded consumers verifying everything that does not use it — and it returns as a **check rather than a selector**, required to agree with what the key type implies. A last resort, and possibly never.
*Revisit* when the root key and the signer key stop sharing a location — an intermediate, a CA service, or a hardware-held root — because that is when signer-only compromise becomes real and revocation starts earning its cost.
*Rejected* keeping the set and sizing an overlap window against the worst expected absence, unbounded in the deployments that most need it and failing to a bricked node; letting an unlisted but CA-vouched signer verify, which is this verdict with a field that then earns nothing; having the outgoing signer sign the incoming certificate, which makes it an intermediate able to mint its own successor; and reserving an empty trust block for a future narrowing ACL over mgmt signers, whose shape would be guesswork carried by every artifact. **Reserve a property, not a field.**
*Rejected* also a **root-signed signer-authorisation artifact**, the shape that looks like an escape from the deadlock of the fourth *Because* above and only moves it: a withdrawal still reaches just the nodes that could already fetch, while the root key enters the path of every authorisation change and signs something that is not a certificate ([ENRL-C0-19](#decision-log)). [ADR-0015](/docs/implementation/adr/0015-anchor-signer-trust-in-root-ca) carries the comparison; C0 has one mgmt signer and the kind check is what binds it.

**ENRL-C0-13. Pin the anchors and the trust domain at the first enrollment, and give C0 no path to change either.**
`identity/anchors.pem` — one file, one or more concatenated PEM certificates, replacing the singular `ca.crt`. It arrives once, at the first enrollment, and is **immutable afterwards**; so is the scope's trust domain. A later bundle delivering a different anchor set or naming a different trust domain is **refused**, compared as set and string equality, alongside the node binding of [ENRL-C0-21](#decision-log). **C0 builds no add path and no remove path.** A rete whose root must change is replaced: new rete, every scope root removed by hand, fresh enrollment everywhere. The set shape is kept because a rotation would need it, not because C0 uses it.
*Because* an anchor change is the one delivery that hands a node a **new root of trust**, and C0 cannot authorise one more strongly than it authorises an ordinary artifact. A re-enrollment manifest is signed by the mgmt signer, so an unpinned anchor would let anyone holding that key replace a node's root permanently — an escalation outliving the leaked certificate's own expiry, and precisely the permanent loss of the management plane that [ADR-0015](/docs/implementation/adr/0015-anchor-signer-trust-in-root-ca) was written to remove. Refusing the change closes it with no mechanism at all.
*Because* the pin forecloses nothing, which is what makes it cheap: C0 has no rotation to break, so the anchors were never going to move inside this milestone. What the pin removes is an *unintended* path to moving them.
*Because* the name had to change with the shape. `ca.crt` reads as singular and says **CA**, which stops being accurate once intermediates exist; `anchors` names the role rather than the kind, and `.pem` is where the plurality shows. Not `trust-bundle`, though that is SPIFFE's term, because **bundle** already names the enrollment artifact and one word cannot carry both.
*Because* **the three cases do not share a mechanism**, which is why one is not designed early. A **planned** rotation is the standard PKI answer — the outgoing root cross-signs the incoming one, both sit in the set through a grace window, the cross-certificate retires once every node holds the successor — and it needs the old key, so it is the only case cross-signature reaches. A **lost** root key cannot cross-sign, so every node receives the successor out of band, which is re-enrolling the fleet. A **compromised** root makes cross-signature actively harmful, since the attacker can cross-sign too, and what is demanded is *removing* the old anchor — for which no channel exists, the one that would carry a retirement being authenticated by the anchor being retired. Naming that gap is the useful part of deferring; a mechanism handling only the planned case would look like a rotation story while covering neither failure that motivates one.
*Because* the deferral is affordable: a root is issued for ten years and a pilot runs for months, and the root key and the signer key share a directory ([ENRL-C0-12](#decision-log)), so root compromise is not a separable event a rotation mechanism could contain.
*Accepting* that the anchors arrive under the out-of-band integrity assumption of a first enrollment and are never re-authorised, so a wrong first anchor is wrong for the life of the scope, with the same manual remedy as everywhere else on this page.
*Accepting* that a future graceful rotation reintroduces the overlap window rejected for signers in [ENRL-C0-12](#decision-log), whose floor is the longest a node may be unreachable. The asymmetry is accepted rather than hidden: a signer rotation is routine and a root rotation is not, so paying a courier for the rare act is a trade the frequent one could not bear.
*Accepting* that **the recursion stops at the root CA**. A compromised root key can cross-sign its own successor and lock the operator out, exactly as a leaked signer key could before ADR-0015 — but it is not a second instance of that defect: below the root CA there was a colder authority available and unused, and above it there is none. An attacker holding the root key already mints any principal at will, so the additional loss is nil. This is the irreducible root.
*Revisit* when a rotation is actually wanted, in B1+ or wherever an intermediate or a CA service changes the problem — an intermediate lets day-to-day issuance rotate without touching an anchor at all, which makes most root rotations unnecessary rather than graceful. The successor owes three things this record does not build: an add path, a remove path, and an authorisation for an anchor change strictly stronger than a mgmt signature.
*Rejected* **seeding a spare anchor at genesis**, which owes none of the three missing paths and still buys less than it costs. It costs a second issuing authority in `retectl init` and a second key with a custody story of its own — kept where the first is not, or it is not a spare. Against that: with no remove path it retires nothing, so a leaked root, or the mgmt signer sharing its directory, still ends in a new rete; and a spare minted at genesis expires alongside the root it doubles, where ten years already outlives any C0 pilot. One case is left — a root key **lost** rather than leaked, a lost signer being recoverable from the root already ([ENRL-C0-12](#decision-log)) — and a backup answers that one better, restoring root, signer and configuration together with no code and no change to any node ([ENRL-C0-34](#decision-log)). It becomes a real option when a remove path exists, at which point it is a rotation's successor rather than a redundant copy.
*Rejected* delivering a first anchor over the mgmt plane, which is the circularity that plane is rooted on; one file per anchor, which would need a pointer or a scan and leave a node trusting a subset of its roots on a partial write, where a concatenated file is replaced by the atomic rename used everywhere else; and letting a re-enrollment bundle deliver a **new** anchor set as an authenticated act, which reads as the anchor-delivery story C0 lacks but is exactly the escalation above — the authority doing the authenticating is the one that must not be able to do this.

**ENRL-C0-40. Say what a scope is in `identity/rete.json`, on both sides of the rete.**
The store's trust-domain record carries the **trust domain always** and the **node name where there is one**: `flor enroll` writes both from the manifest's `trust_domain` and `node` claims, and they are what a re-enrollment's pin is compared against ([ENRL-C0-13](#decision-log), [ENRL-C0-21](#decision-log)). `retectl init` writes the same file in the operator's tree at `~/.retectl/retes/<scope>/identity/rete.json`, with the trust domain and **no node** — an operator's tree belongs to a rete and not to a machine in it. **Every holder reads its own trust domain out of that record and none derives it from a path**, which is the whole of what the operator-side copy is for: one library serving both stores, with one way of answering the question. The division with [ENRL-C0-39](#decision-log)'s receipt is that **`rete.json` says what the scope is and `receipt.json` says what the last enroll did**, and neither repeats the other.
*Because* **a pin needs somewhere to be pinned.** ENRL-C0-13 refuses a bundle whose trust domain or node differs from the scope's, and ENRL-C0-21 refuses one carrying another node's identity, but a comparison needs both sides: the trust domain had a home and the node had none, so the node binding — the one with the everyday failure behind it — was a rule with nothing to check against.
*Because* **`identity/` is already the place a holder answers questions about itself**, and the rule that consumers *read* the trust domain there rather than sampling SPIFFE IDs out of configs ([Agent § Artifact handling](../agent#artifact-handling)) is the same rule one segment further along. A second file for the second half would be an enumeration where a record already exists, which is the failure mode [ENRL-C0-21](#decision-log)'s one-subtree argument is about.
*Because* **the store shape is supposed to serve both sides with different parts populated** ([ENRL-C0-06](#decision-log)), and this is what makes that true of the record as well as of the directories: a node fills `node` and an operator does not, exactly as a node's `authorities/` is empty and an operator's `anchors.pem` absent. Optionality is the store's existing idiom, not a concession.
*Because* **a trust domain read off a directory name is derived from the filesystem, and this design does not derive it from anything.** Without the record `retectl` would have to take the scope directory's name as the trust domain — the one fact in the identity model that [Agent § Artifact handling](../agent#artifact-handling) says is *read* and never reconstructed. A path is a worse source than a config, not a better one: directory names carry the filesystem-safe constraints that page calls a documented C0 shortcut rather than architecture — case-folding on macOS and Windows, length limits, a reserved-basename list — and the designed fix is a store shim whose file backend maps names through a reversible encoding, at which point the directory name **stops being** the trust domain and every reader that took it for one is wrong. The record is immune to that, and it is what lets one library answer the question the same way on both sides.
*Because* **a store should say what it is**, so a tree restored from backup — the remedy [ENRL-C0-34](#decision-log) now names for a lost root key — announces its rete rather than depending on the directory it was restored into.
*Accepting* that the operator-side record **guards nothing, and cannot** — it is a source, not a check, and the earlier reading of it as a rename guard was wrong. `retectl` resolves the scope *from* `rete.name` ([ENRL-C0-08](#decision-log)), so an edited `rete.name` sends it looking under a directory that does not exist rather than at a record that would disagree: the value being checked is the value the lookup is keyed by, and the trust domain is already pinned by being the directory's name. What the operator sees on a rename is a missing tree, which is a refusal of a duller kind, and C0 leaves it there. Comparing the record against `rete.yaml` remains worth doing and catches only a tree moved by hand — worth its one line, and not a guard.
*Accepting* that the file gains an optional field, so a consumer reading `node` must handle its absence rather than assuming a node wrote the file. That is the cost of one record over two, and it is paid once in a parser.
*Accepting* that a **scope root's node name is written before the scope is committed**, since `rete.json` is part of the identity plane and the receipt's `enrolled` comes after it. An interrupted run therefore leaves a node name that pins nothing, which is correct and is [ENRL-C0-39](#decision-log)'s rule rather than an exception to it: the phase decides, not the material.
*Revisit* what else the record should carry when a scope holds more than one trust domain. Interrete adds further domains to the same anchor set, and a record shaped for exactly one is the first thing that stops fitting.
*Rejected* a separate `node.json` beside `rete.json`, which splits one question — who am I, and in what domain — across two files and needs its own creation and ordering rule; putting the node name in [ENRL-C0-39](#decision-log)'s receipt, which would make an identity fact a property of the run that happened to write it, and would mean the agent reads a *receipt* to learn its own name; deriving the node from `identity/principals/`, which cannot say which of several leaves is the node principal; and leaving the operator's tree without the record, letting `retectl` read the trust domain off the scope directory's name, which is the path-derived fact this entry exists to remove and would give the shared store two ways to answer one question.

**ENRL-C0-14. Say *root CA* for the authority and *trust anchor* for what a verifier trusts.**
Never a bare "CA". The **root CA** is an issuing authority — it signs certificates, and its private key is what an operator protects. A **trust anchor** is what a relying party validates against, and `identity/anchors.pem` is the node's set of them. The two usually coincide and are not the same thing: a deployment can anchor on an intermediate, and an intermediate is a CA that is not an anchor.
*Because* "the CA" is unambiguous only while there is exactly one and it is the root, which is true now and is the first thing to stop being true. Fixing the vocabulary before the taxonomy grows costs a sweep; fixing it afterwards costs a sweep plus every reader who learned the loose usage. This is standard PKI usage rather than an invention, which is most of its recommendation.
*Accepting* that the sweep crosses the CLI, identity and source-layout pages and the signing-trust and object-model records.

**ENRL-C0-15. Join by a per-node bundle carrying the node's initial artifact set.**
One bundle per node enrolls that machine and every principal on it. The operator generates the keypairs (Flow A); the principal-generated CSR path (Flow B) is not part of C0. The bundle carries the node's initial mgmt set.
**Enrollment therefore works offline, and that is an invariant rather than a happy accident**: no coordinator reachability is required at enroll time, the bundle carrying the set, `incoming/` being consumed before the first dial and the first sync being opportunistic.
*Because* a server hosting five services would otherwise need five deliveries, five bearer files and five windows to close, for one machine that is trusted or not as a unit; because during pilots the operator *is* the Florete team, so the trust Flow A asks for already exists in fact; and because the management node cannot fetch artifacts from a coordinator that is not yet running — its own `coordinator.json` is in that set — while no node has Florete connectivity before it enrolls.
*Because* the offline property is what makes provisioning a node at a depot identical to provisioning a server.
*Accepting* that this is C0's join model and explicitly not the design's final form: a token-and-fetch join, a principal-generated key, and a two-phase split with fleet-common material are successors, not rejected alternatives.
*Revisit* the artifact-set clause under a two-phase installer, where it does **not** change — the set is node-specific by construction, so what a split moves is the anchor.

**ENRL-C0-37. Keep the two mgmt channels independent, and never let the bundle depend on the coordinator.**
mgmt state reaches a node two ways: the **coordinator**, online and incremental and carrying artifacts alone; and the **bundle**, out of band and manual and carrying identity material as well. Neither may be a precondition of the other. Concretely: nothing on the bundle path — issuing, delivering or enrolling — may require the coordinator to be reachable, to be current, or to have worked at all. The bundle is the more capable of the two and it is the repair path for the other's failure.
*Because* the failure that most needs the bundle is the coordinator's own. A dead management node is repaired by re-enrolling it or its replacement, which needs a bundle; if cutting one needed a publish, the repair for a dead coordinator would need a working coordinator. **That is a cycle, and it is genesis recurring after a failure** — except genesis can be excused as a first act and a rebuild cannot.
*Because* the asymmetry is already load-bearing elsewhere: [ENRL-C0-15](#decision-log) makes needing no coordinator reachability an invariant of enrollment rather than a convenience, and [ENRL-C0-21](#decision-log) reads `incoming/` before the first dial precisely so a node that cannot reach the coordinator can still be repaired. A dependency at the issuing end would undo both from the other side.
*Accepting* that **the two channels converge at different speeds, and revocation's effective time is the slowest node's**. C0 revocation is a name removal that every node applies at its next sync ([ENRL-C0-24](#decision-log)); a node fed mainly by courier keeps admitting a revoked principal until it syncs, and nothing shortens that. Independence buys the repair path and pays for it in convergence.
*Accepting* that transition windows widen with it — a change is minutes wide for coordinator-fed nodes and courier-wide for the others ([ENRL-C0-38](#decision-log)).
*Rejected* a single channel, which is the same as deleting the repair path; and any rule making one channel's use conditional on the other's health, of which gating issuance on publish was the instance this entry was written against ([ENRL-C0-35](#decision-log)).

**ENRL-C0-16. Ship the bundle as a versioned tar container of three separable parts.**
`<node>.bundle` is a tar archive holding `manifest.json`, `anchors.pem`, `credentials/<leaf>.crt` and `.key` — the principal's SPIFFE leaf, not its full ID — and `artifacts/<plane>/<name>.json` byte-exact as the compiler emitted them. The manifest carries its own `signature` block, in the shape and canonicalisation an envelope uses. Entry names are **logical identifiers, never destinations, and no filename is an input to any path**: a credential's destination is the leaf of the SPIFFE ID read out of the certificate itself, a key is paired to its certificate by **public-key correspondence** rather than by name (and uses the same leaf as destination name), and artifact is deposited for the agent's intake by destination out of the envelope. Digests are **SHA-256** with no algorithm field. **Names must still agree with contents**, as a producer check rather than a source: a credential entry's `<leaf>` must equal the certificate's and corresponding key's, an artifact's entry's `<plane>` and `<name>` segments the `plane` and `name` in the envelope it carries, every certificate's trust domain and every artifact envelope's `node` the manifest's. **Six refusals keep the archive unambiguous**: a name disagreeing with its content, a repeated entry name, two certificates carrying the same leaf, a key matching no certificate, an entry that is not a regular file, and a manifest-named entry the archive does not carry; an entry the manifest does not name is not extracted, and a certificate with no key is the keyless bundle rather than an error. The manifest's `trust_domain` and `node` are claims under its signature rather than facts read back out of entries, because they are two of the three bindings [ENRL-C0-13](#decision-log) pins.
**`schema_version` makes the manifest a fourth versioned surface** under [ADR-0012](/docs/implementation/adr/0012-version-artifact-contract), gated same-major and minor-older-or-equal, and stamped by `issue-bundle` at the lowest minor its content uses.
*Because* the three parts must stay structurally distinct even though C0 always ships all three: a keyless credential set, an anchor delivered by another channel, and a fleet-common part each become possible without a format change, and three separate futures ask for exactly that.
*Because* entry names are logical where the producer and consumer **can skew across releases**, so a bundle written against one scope-root layout must not break against another. And a container beats a document because a signed envelope embedded in JSON is either a nested object whose bytes change on re-serialisation or an escaped string, while a tar entry is the bytes.
*Because* **minimum-minor stamping is what makes that skew survivable**: without it a newer `retectl` would stamp its newest minor on every bundle and strand every older `flor`, which is the ordinary direction here — an operator upgrades on their schedule and a node on its own.
*Because* **tar lets an archive say a thing twice, and a fresh enrollment authenticates none of it.** Duplicate names are legal on tape and readers disagree about which wins, so a digest checked against one occurrence and a file written from another is a check that passes over bytes nobody verified; a repeated name is therefore a refusal, not a resolution. Non-regular entries are refused for the same reason destinations are derived — a symlink or a device node is an entry type whose *effect* is not the file it names. And a manifest-named entry the archive lacks would otherwise leave a principal silently unmaterialised on an enrollment that reported success.
*Because* **a destination derived from a filename is not derived at all, and a pairing made by filename is a binding nobody checked.** `identity/principals/<leaf>` with `<leaf>` taken off the entry is a path the bundle chose, safe only behind a charset test — a shape check standing in for a fact, on a first enrollment where nothing has been authenticated. Reading the leaf out of the certificate's SAN needs no such test: the SPIFFE parse enforces the charset itself ([ADR-0005](/docs/implementation/adr/0005-use-spiffe-identities)), so a surviving leaf is a path component by construction, and the path says what the material *is* rather than what the archive called it. The key follows the same move, and only there does the check reach something a name never could: a bundle cut from a stale tree can hand a node one principal's certificate beside another's private key with every filename looking right, and comparing the key's public counterpart against the certificate's `SubjectPublicKeyInfo` is what makes that unrepresentable. One rule — **destination and pairing from content, never from transport** — and the identity plane now obeys the one the artifact plane already did.
*Because* **the collision moved with the derivation.** A duplicate entry name used to be a duplicate destination, so one rule covered both; now two differently-named entries can carry certificates with the same leaf. The refusal is therefore on the **derived destination** — leaf names being cross-kind unique by validator rule, an honest bundle cannot produce one — and the duplicate-name rule stays for what it actually guards, a digest checked against one occurrence and a file written from another.
*Accepting* a tar dependency, a manifest to keep consistent with the archive, and an X.509 parse before the first write — which the flow was paying for anyway, every written certificate being checked for expiry ([ENRL-C0-33](#decision-log)). Path traversal is then closed by extracting only manifest-named entries to destinations no filename contributed to: a structural closure rather than a filter, and not a sentence to weaken into "the names are validated".
*Accepting* that a second digest algorithm is a `schema_version`-gated schema change when it arrives — the property is reserved, not a field.
*Rejected* validating each entry name as a single path component and keeping it as the source, a shape test in place of a fact that leaves the certificate-key pairing unchecked; and naming credential entries by full SPIFFE ID, which puts a trust domain and a kind in a filename where the certificate already carries both.

**ENRL-C0-17. Verify a bundle only against trust the node already holds.**
A fresh enrollment verifies **no signature**. It checks every entry against the manifest's digests, then displays the trust domain, the anchors' fingerprints and the signer's name, marked unverified — a display, and no flag turns it into a check. A **re-enrollment** validates the certificate the manifest carries against the anchors the node already holds, then checks the signature, refusing a signer that does not chain; and it compares the delivered anchor set and trust domain against what the scope is pinned to, refusing on any difference ([ENRL-C0-13](#decision-log)).
*Because* a signature checked against a key the bundle itself supplies proves nothing: an attacker who alters a bundle re-signs it and updates the trust material it delivers. Removing that check leaves **exactly one verification path**, which can only be pointed at keys the node already trusts — and a path that cannot be handed an attacker-supplied key is worth more than one guarded by care. A manifest carrying the key alongside its name would put a verifier one convenience away from exactly that bug.
*Because* the comparison is what makes the pin real. Verifying a re-enrollment against the anchors proves the manifest was signed by someone the node trusts; it says nothing about what the bundle *delivers*, and without the comparison a signer could hand a node a different root. The two are separate checks against the same material and both are needed.
*Accepting* that unverified fresh enrollment is the default and remains possible; that a bundle with a malformed signature enrolls and fails only at the first re-enrollment; and that the operator's reading of the displayed fingerprints is advisory, catching an honest mix-up rather than an attacker.
*Accepting* that a **leaked** signer key is unrecoverable in C0 for the reasons in [ENRL-C0-12](#decision-log), and that a **lost** one is not a scope-root problem at all: a fresh certificate minted from the root restores re-enrollment without touching any node.
*Rejected* an `--anchor-fingerprint` flag comparing the displayed set against operator-supplied values. It looked like the non-interactive half of a human check, but the operator who could supply the expected fingerprints is the operator who cut the bundle, so it puts a second channel in every pilot's critical path for a confirmation that party cannot independently make; and the threat it was reaching for — a substituted anchor set — is closed structurally by the pin at every enrollment after the first, where it is a refusal rather than a flag anyone must remember. Also rejected: checking that the signer appears in the trust material the bundle delivers, which is circular in the same way the fresh-enrollment signature check is.

**ENRL-C0-18. Bound the bearer window nowhere at the bundle level.**
C0 enforces nothing on the interval between issue and enroll. Guidance covers it: deliver over a channel the recipient trusts, enroll promptly, delete the file. The credential's own validity is the only bound that exists.
**`flor enroll` deletes the bundle on success**, or prints a loud instruction where it cannot — hygiene rather than a control, but the honest path's leftover copy is the only one C0 can act on at all.
*Because* a control checked by our own consumer binds **only the honest party**. An attacker holding a bundle does not run `flor enroll`; they hold PEM keys and certificates and copy them into place. Anything that binds an attacker must be checked by someone they must convince — a **peer** at the handshake, or an **issuer** at fetch.
*Accepting* that C0 ships with no enforceable bearer-window control, stated plainly rather than papered over.
*Revisit* when a credential can be spent at first use, which replaces expiry with consumption.
*Rejected* a bundle expiry, on the argument above; single-use redemption, which needs an issuer that remembers a principal has enrolled — and the coordinator deliberately keeps no per-node state.

### Genesis and the node-side act

**ENRL-C0-19. Keep the root CA, the mgmt signer and the operator's identity separate.**
Three distinct things. The CA is the issuing authority and not a principal; it signs certificates and never envelopes. The mgmt signer is a signing principal whose key is cold, lives on operator hardware, and is loaded by no vertex. The operator's identity is an ordinary TLS principal, bundle-delivered and revocable like anyone's. `primary` names a **slot** rather than a description, and rotation keeps the name: since a node matches a signer by certificate and never by a persisted name ([ENRL-C0-12](#decision-log)), a fresh certificate is minted for `primary` and the old pair replaced — no successor name, and no second `signers.mgmt.keys` entry.
*Because* a signing identity must not be revocable by an HR event — merging it with the operator's user entry means revoking a departing operator breaks artifact verification for the whole rete. And signing envelopes with the root key would put it in the path of every compile, which is the one place it must never be.

**ENRL-C0-20. Keep activation out of enroll.**
`flor enroll` unpacks and stops there; **`flor agent enable` is the only thing that activates**, with `disable` as its inverse. Enrolling a node is therefore two commands rather than one command with two steps, and enroll prints the `enable` that finishes the job. Uninstall reverses activation and leaves the scope root.
**"Activate" names an act, never a service manager.** Today it instantiates the installer-placed wrapper; a nested rete would make it a supervision entry instead, and the contract prose must not foreclose that by naming systemd.
*Because* the alternative charges a **re-issued bundle** for a reinstall — bearer material couriered out of band for a node that already holds valid identity — and because a multi-rete node must be able to leave one rete without touching the others. `disable` is neither revocation nor purge.
*Because* **enroll cannot both hold the scope lock and start the party that needs it** ([ENRL-C0-39](#decision-log)). Activating while holding it deadlocks; releasing first would put enroll's last act outside the lock it took to make the act safe. Handing activation to `enable` removes the choice rather than picking the less bad half.
*Because* **the operator is already there**. `enroll` is a couriered-bundle act with a person or a script at the keyboard, and the same caller runs `enable` on the next line; the cost is one command in a sequence that already has five.
*Accepting* that a re-keyed node **stays down until someone enables it**, where enroll used to bring it back by itself. The mitigation is the installer's: enroll's last line prints the exact `flor agent enable` for the scope ([Installer § Install](../../installer#install) reports the same way), and a node that is enrolled-but-inactive is a documented resting state rather than a failure.
*Accepting* that "enroll in two steps" stops describing enroll and starts describing the operator's sequence. What the split was actually protecting is untouched: uninstall still reverses activation alone, and a scope root still resumes with no bundle.
*Revisit* an activating flag, or a `stop`/`start` pair beside `enable`/`disable`, when something wants a takedown that survives a reboot. C0 needs neither: the receipt makes a reboot mid-enroll safe, since the agent refuses a scope reading `enrolling`.
*Rejected* enroll activating and enroll stopping, both for the lock reason above; and a `--no-start` escape, which is this decision as a flag and therefore two paths where one will do.
*Rejected* a per-scope removal command. Several remedies on this page end in removing a scope root by hand — a leaked signer key, moving a scope to a different node, replacing a rete whose root must change, and a partial root whose bundle is gone — and C0 still ships nothing for it: `flor uninstall --purge` removes everything and `disable` removes nothing. A pilot is not expected to need it, and a command nobody exercises is a command nobody has tested. The pages say "by hand" and mean it.

**ENRL-C0-21. Enroll by depositing, and re-enroll by replacing only what the bundle names.**
Enroll verifies before it writes anything, refuses while the scope is live rather than stopping it ([ENRL-C0-39](#decision-log)), then writes the identity plane — replacing the material of **each principal the bundle names and leaving every other principal's untouched** — and **deposits** the artifact files in `incoming/` rather than staging them. `agent/` and `workloads/` survive. A re-enrollment is refused when the bundle differs from what the scope is pinned to in any of the three claims of [ENRL-C0-13](#decision-log). A failed run converges rather than resuming a particular bundle, and the receipt and lock that make that work are [ENRL-C0-39](#decision-log)'s. The sequence is the Shape's `enroll` block.
*Because* a bundle-delivered set is another intake path, and treating it as one keeps monotonicity a rule rather than an exception: writing it straight into `artifacts/` would install a stale set unchecked and reopen the replay window the version mark exists to close. **Staging is the agent's** — it is part of its intake and belongs in one place — and enroll runs before the agent does, so a handover is needed either way. Making it a drop of files rather than a call into set machinery keeps the boundary narrow. It is consumed **before the first dial** because that is the whole of the repair path: a node offline long enough that the coordinator moved cannot reach it to learn where it went, so a set arriving only after a successful sync arrives never.
*Because* the authoritative statement of which principals live on a node is the **artifact set**, not the bundle. A bundle is a delivery, so reading its contents as a census makes a subset bundle destructive — invisible in C0, whose bundles carry everything, and wrong the moment a rotation delivers one principal's material.
*Because* an agent reading the identity plane while enroll rewrites it is a race — closed by the scope lock rather than by a stop, so correctness rests on the lock and not on the operator having read the step ([ENRL-C0-39](#decision-log)).
*Accepting* that re-enrollment is not atomic and cannot be — renaming over a populated directory is atomic nowhere, and part of that directory must survive; what stands in for atomicity is [ENRL-C0-39](#decision-log)'s convergence, one mechanism for both cases, since a node is enrolled once and re-enrolled whenever it is re-keyed.
*Accepting* that **`flor enroll` succeeds before the set it delivered has been admitted**, and cannot report what the agent will make of it. The gap is real: on a fresh enrollment no artifact signature has been checked at all, so a set signed by a key that does not chain to the anchors the same bundle delivered enrolls cleanly and is rejected only at intake — and the bundle is already deleted, though it could not have helped, its content being malformed in this case. What C0 owes this is noise rather than a mechanism.
*Accepting* that the two non-admissions are different events: a set **ignored as older** is expected, since a bundle's set legitimately goes stale between issue and enroll, and logs at INFO; a set **rejected as invalid** logs at WARN once, at the admission attempt. The slot is removed either way, and nothing on the node records that it was rejected.
*Accepting* that material for a principal removed from YAML since the last bundle is left in place rather than swept. It opens nothing, no peer's access check naming that principal.
*Revisit* the sweep when a bundle can legitimately carry a subset. It is deferred for a reason that is not tidiness — **artifacts are re-fetchable and identity material is not** — so an over-eager sweep is unrecoverable where the artifact cleanup pass is merely wasteful; it would have to run only after a set activates, never touch the node principal, and survive a momentarily-wrong set.
*Revisit* enroll performing the intake itself when `incoming/` proves a source of silent failures in practice rather than in principle; reading `flor agent`'s status before completing would be the better shape, and C0 defers it.
*Rejected* replacing the whole identity subtree, correct today and wrong later for a reason that will not announce itself; starting the agent and handing the set over its control socket, which inverts the enroll-then-activate order and needs a socket that may not ship; enroll performing the intake through a shared library function, which would make its success truthful and puts set construction and the version mark in a second caller to buy a better error message; and keeping a rejected slot as evidence, which preserves bytes nobody can re-admit in a directory scanned at every start.

**ENRL-C0-39. Make a partial enrollment converge, with a receipt for the phase and one lock for the scope.**
`retes/<scope>/receipt.json` carries exactly two things: the **scope root's `schema_version`** and the enrollment **phase**, `enrolling` or `enrolled`. It is written before anything else lands in the scope root and replaced with `enrolled` as the last act of enroll, which makes that replacement the **commit point** — the pin of [ENRL-C0-13](#decision-log) becomes real there and not before. The phase decides which path a run takes: **no receipt is a fresh enrollment**, **`enrolling` is still fresh** because nothing has been pinned yet, and **`enrolled` is a re-enrollment**. `retes/<scope>/scope.lock` is the scope's single advisory lock, held by the agent for as long as it serves and by `flor enroll` for its whole run; a holder is named rather than queued for, and the name enroll prints is the `flor agent disable` that clears the ordinary one. **Enroll takes it before it reads anything**: creating `retes/` and the scope root comes first, because the lock lives inside the scope root; everything after — the receipt, the anchors, the pinned claims — is read under the lock and acted on under the same one. **Enroll releases it upon exit and activates nothing** ([ENRL-C0-20](#decision-log)). The agent reads the receipt before serving and **refuses a scope whose receipt is absent, reads `enrolling`, or carries a `schema_version` this release cannot understand**, naming the way out in each case. There is **no bundle identity on disk**: any bundle this scope would otherwise accept completes an interrupted run, and `incoming/` is replaced wholesale rather than merged.
*Because* **every check enroll performs reads scope state, so a read outside the lock is a decision something else may already have invalidated.** The receipt chooses the path, the anchors verify the manifest, the pinned claims decide whether the bundle applies at all — none is a property of the bundle alone, and a concurrent agent or a second enroll can move any of them between the read and the write. Taking the lock first collapses that window instead of narrowing it, which is why the ordering is scope-then-bundle and not the reverse the flow reads more naturally as.
*Because* the phase is the only thing that can decide fresh from re-enrolled, and the material cannot.
*Because* written first with a phase, a receipt states a fact rather than an intention — the installer's rule ([Installer § Install](../../installer#install)), and it is what makes a crashed run recognisable as ours instead of as a mystery tree.
*Because* the lock has to be the scope's rather than enroll's. A lock belonging to enroll would not exclude the agent, which a service manager can restart mid-write, and that is the collision that corrupts an identity plane. One lock every owner of the scope takes closes it, and it is the same guarantee the agent already wanted for itself.
*Because* a held flock dies with its holder, so a killed enroll or a power loss strands nothing and leaves no file for anyone to clear by hand. The file's existence means nothing; the held lock is the lock.
*Because* the version gates the scope root, not the receipt. What a downgrade risks is an older `flor agent run` reading `identity/`, `artifacts/` and `agent/`, and [Installer § Downgrade](../../installer#downgrade) already reserved this refusal for the agent on exactly that ground — the installer cannot judge whether an older binary can serve a node's state, and the agent can. The number gates by [ADR-0012](/docs/implementation/adr/0012-version-artifact-contract)'s one rule, own major and minor-older-or-equal, so a plain downgrade reaches releases sharing it and no further.
*Because* a persisted artifact outlives the process that wrote it, which is the whole reason it needs a version at all. Producer and consumer are one release within a single run and not across a crash and a retry: an operator whose enroll died and who reaches for the previous release is the ordinary way an older binary meets a newer scope root.
*Accepting* the lock has to sit in the scope root, not the runtime root. The agent's per-scope lock lived in the runtime root and the agent removes that root on a clean stop ([Agent § Local IPC](../agent#local-ipc)), so stopping the agent before enrolling would delete the directory holding the lock it is about to take. The scope lock in the scope root exists whenever the scope does.
*Accepting* that enroll no longer promises to finish the run it started, only to converge. Leftover material from an interrupted run is inert by [ENRL-C0-21](#decision-log)'s rule, and `incoming/` is replaced rather than merged, so what survives a crash is stale material and never a mixture.
*Accepting* that a crash can leave a **torn pair** — one bundle's certificate beside another's key — since two files cannot be renamed as one. Each is written to a temporary name and renamed in, so every file is old-or-new; the pair is repaired by the next run, and the public-key correspondence of [ENRL-C0-16](#decision-log) is what names the state rather than leaving it to a failed handshake.
*Accepting* that the agent gains a runtime dependency on a file `flor enroll` writes, and that the scope root gains a version that never fires in C0, nothing having migrated yet. Its value is that the first migration finds the refusal already in place instead of inventing it.
*Accepting* that enroll **refuses a live scope rather than quieting it**, which is one more command for the operator and no window at all for anyone: a stop-then-acquire sequence has a gap a service manager can restart the agent into, where a refusal has none. The person who ran `enroll` expects the scope down for a material change in any case.
*Accepting* that a refused bundle can leave an **empty scope root** behind on a first enrollment, the container having been created before the bundle was read. It carries no receipt, so it is exactly a scope that has not been enrolled — inert to the agent, and completed by the next run rather than needing a cleanup.
*Revisit* whether `enable`/`disable` should be joined by a `stop`/`start` pair, once something wants an agent down without deregistering it. C0 does not: `disable` is the only takedown, and a reboot inside an enroll is safe because the agent refuses a scope whose receipt reads `enrolling`.
*Revisit* the bundle's identity on disk when something must **decide** on content rather than report it. The digest is cheap to add and earns nothing today; the caller that would want it is the set-identity question of [ENRL-C0-35](#decision-log), which is a design rather than a field.
*Rejected* **keying the partial scope root to the bundle that made it** — recording the manifest digest and refusing a different bundle, which is what this entry replaces. It blocks no attacker, since a fresh enrollment verifies nothing and anyone who can drop a bundle can remove the partial root first, while on a re-enrollment anything reaching the write stage has already passed the signature and the three pinned claims. What it does block is an operator who re-cut the bundle, and their way out is a hand-removal C0 ships no command for ([ENRL-C0-20](#decision-log)).
*Rejected* recording the digest for a **warning** alone, which buys a sentence nobody can act on differently — that the run is completing an interrupted one is the whole of the actionable fact; a separate `enroll.lock`, on the agent-exclusion argument above; leaving the lock in the runtime root; and versioning the receipt rather than the scope root, which would let a receipt field block a downgrade the trees were perfectly happy with.

**ENRL-C0-22. Create `retes/` from one library function, whichever component runs first.**
`retes/` is `0755`, owned by root in system mode and by the invoking user in user mode; `retes/<scope>/` is `0700`, owned by the service user. Both the installer and `flor enroll` create it through the same function.
*Because* creating the directory is not writing **into** it — the installer's prohibition is about contents, which are the agent's, and its permissions rule is about the container. Neither component is guaranteed to run: a distribution package places binaries without invoking `flor install`. A rule stated in two places is a rule that eventually becomes two rules.

**ENRL-C0-23. Owe every human-facing check a non-interactive equivalent, and note that C0 has none.**
Enrollment over SSH, into a provisioning script, or at a machine's first boot is the same path with the same checks. Where a check's interactive form is a person reading something off a screen, its machine form is a flag, and automation is never made to **skip** a check in order to run unattended. **C0 ships no check of that kind.** Everything `flor enroll` verifies is mechanical and identical either way — the digests, the signature on a re-enrollment, and the comparisons against the pinned trust domain, anchors and node. What an operator sees before a first enrollment is a display, not a decision.
*Because* two flows would diverge and the unattended one would be the less-exercised and less-safe of the pair, which is how unattended paths quietly become the insecure ones. The rule is worth keeping without an instance: it is a constraint on the next check anyone proposes, and the one place it would have bitten in C0 was resolved by removing the human from the loop rather than by adding a flag.
*Revisit* when a check genuinely needs a person, at which point this entry supplies the shape and stops being a rule with an empty extension.
*Rejected* dropping the rule along with `--anchor-fingerprint`, its only former instance ([ENRL-C0-17](#decision-log)): the flag was rejected on its own merits, not because the principle failed.

**ENRL-C0-36. Treat missing material for a declared principal as degraded, never as an error.**
An artifact set naming a **local** principal whose `identity/principals/<leaf>.crt|.key` is absent is a normal steady state. The vertex starts, serves every principal it has material for, and does not serve that one — **no listener at all**, rather than a listener that cannot complete a handshake — warning once per principal at load. Only local principals can be in this state: an `ingress.allow` entry naming a remote one needs no local material. The mirror already holds, [ENRL-C0-21](#decision-log) leaving a removed principal's material in place and inert. So: **material without an artifact is inert; an artifact without material is degraded; both are normal.**
*Because* [ENRL-C0-35](#decision-log) makes the window standard rather than rare — the set ships at publish and the credentials arrive by courier, so every added principal spends the courier's duration in exactly this state. A condition that occurs on every change of this kind cannot be modelled as a fault.
*Because* refusing the set would let the **identity store veto the artifact set**, inverting [ENRL-C0-21](#decision-log)'s rule that the set is what is authoritative about which principals live on a node — and it would block unrelated changes, one missing credential stopping four working services.
*Because* the **vertex is already the component that resolves identity**: it loads a principal's material by convention and verifies the certified ID matches the declared one, so *absent* is that same check's other outcome, on material only it reads. The agent stays payload-blind and the boundary between them gains nothing.
*Accepting* that a service can be published, routable and simply not answering, with the reason visible only in that node's log — and that **`retectl` cannot warn about it**, since issuance is unrecorded ([ENRL-C0-24](#decision-log)), so nothing operator-side can compare what is declared against what has been issued.
*Revisit* with the issuance ledger of [ENRL-C0-24](#decision-log), which would make that comparison possible — "this node's published set names a principal whose material has never been issued" — and which is a stronger motivation for the ledger than the expiry answer recorded there.
*Rejected* refusing the whole set until every named principal has material, on the veto and blast-radius arguments above; refusing to start the vertex, which is the same objection at a coarser grain; and a listener that accepts and then fails the handshake, which presents an absent service to its caller as a trust failure.


### Revoking and rotating

**ENRL-C0-24. Revoke by removing a principal, and accept that it costs the name.**
The operator removes the principal from YAML, compiles and publishes; every node drops it from its ingress tables at the next sync. There is no CRL and nothing is pushed. Git records a principal's **declaration and removal** and nothing else — **issuance is unrecorded in C0**. Expiry is surfaced by [ENRL-C0-33](#decision-log) and bound by nothing; the operator's own reminder covers the rest.
*Because* the revoked principal disappears from every peer's access check, so a live private key opens nothing. What that conceals is the cost: C0 does not have a revocation mechanism, it has a **name change**, and re-admitting the same person or service means every ACL, group and role referencing the old name is rewritten.
*Accepting* that the page says both of those in those words rather than implying git covers issuance.
*Revisit* with an operator-side issuance ledger, committed to the repo — which answers "what expires soon" without asking any node, is visible to a second operator in a way a private tree would not be, and is the enrollment record this milestone lacks. Its stronger motivation arrived with [ENRL-C0-36](#decision-log): the ledger is the only thing that could let the operator side compare what is **declared** against what has been **issued**, and so notice a published set naming a principal whose material has never been cut. Expiry is the lesser of the two answers it gives.
*Rejected* burning the name as the permanent design; a distributed revocation list, deferred elsewhere for reasons that stand; short lifetimes alone, which bound exposure without ever letting anyone *act*.

**ENRL-C0-25. Keep rotation policy no finer than the rotation mechanism.**
One rete-wide `tls_principals.validity_days`, and rotation at the granularity of a node, because a bundle is per node. Cadence granularity may become finer exactly when rotation granularity does.
**And no certificate may outlive the root that issued it — so every mint clamps rather than refuses.** The three per-category lifetimes are independent knobs, so a leaf minted late in the root's life would carry a `notAfter` beyond its anchor's; the chain then fails at the **root's** expiry while the leaf still looks valid — fleet-wide, and after the material is couriered and installed. Every command that mints therefore sets `notAfter` to the **earlier** of the requested lifetime and the root's own, and warns when it clamped, naming both dates: `retectl init`, `retectl id issue` and `retectl issue-bundle`. `compile` and `issue-bundle` still check the invariant and refuse on a violation, which after clamping can only mean material minted outside this tooling. A clamp leaving no usable validity — a root already expired, or expiring within the day — is the one refusal, since there is nothing left to issue.
*Because* a per-class policy is meaningless while the only mechanism is a whole-node bundle: setting a service to thirty days and a user to seven changes nothing when both are re-keyed together by the same courier, and it is worse than useless, since it states an intent the system does not honour.
*Accepting* that C0's ninety days is a placeholder with a stated reason rather than a considered value. The criterion for a real number is unusual here and belongs on the page: **a credential's lifetime must exceed the longest period its holder may be unable to reach an issuer.** Hour-long certificates are free for users on corporate networks and wrong for a device out of contact for a week, which would brick itself for reasons unrelated to any attack.
*Accepting* that ninety days is therefore also a **bound on a C0 pilot**, since a courier is the only renewal: a pilot that outlives its credentials costs a visit to every node. [C0 Scope](../../scope#use-case) states it as a limitation rather than leaving it to be discovered — C0's nodes are desktops and servers an operator can reach, and its pilots are planned to end inside the window.
*Accepting* that a clamped credential is **shorter than policy**, silently to everyone but the operator who saw the warning — and that this is the intended reading: the credential is as long as the rete's own remaining life, which is the true bound. Its holder is warned proportionally, [ENRL-C0-33](#decision-log)'s threshold being a fraction of *that certificate's* validity rather than of the policy it asked for. What the clamp makes visible is the approach of the root's `notAfter` — leaves shortening across the fleet, rather than issuance working normally and then stopping.
*Rejected* per-class and per-principal cadence as policy ahead of mechanism; **refusing the mint** rather than clamping it, which would stop the rete issuing anything for the last `tls_principals.validity_days` of its root's life — the window in which an operator most needs to re-key, with no root rotation to reach for ([ENRL-C0-13](#decision-log)) — and turns a gradual bound into a cliff; and a bare **warning** with no clamp, which leaves the invariant unenforced and defers the failure to the fleet, after the material is couriered and installed. The existing per-category fields are admissible because the root CA, the signers and the TLS principals genuinely rotate by different means.

**ENRL-C0-26. Target bounded exposure, and be precise about what healing is available.**
A compromised credential is useful for a bounded time because credentials expire. That is a deadline, not healing, and is never described as if it were. Two further statements are kept apart from it and from each other.
**What post-compromise security actually claims** — healing after a credential leak, against an adversary who does not interfere afterwards — is reachable here, **opportunistically**, and ENRL-C0-28 is the mechanism. What is not reachable is that property against a peer the node has never met, because a certificate handshake carries nothing from any previous one and there is no state to heal; a peer with history is a different case, and the anchor covers the rest.
**An attacker with continuing presence on the node** is outside that model and outside this design's. No rotation or continuity scheme addresses it: *rotation defends against copies, never against presence.*
*Because* forward secrecy is often mistaken for this and is the opposite direction — it protects past sessions against future compromise. And an unqualified claim in either direction is worse than none: saying post-compromise security is unreachable would deny what ENRL-C0-28 delivers, while claiming it plainly would promise healing against an adversary who is still there.
*Accepting* that C0 itself has neither: a re-enrolled node's material is only as fresh as the operator's generator, and an attacker holding old material and observing the courier channel is not excluded by re-enrollment.

**ENRL-C0-27. Replace a credential over the mgmt channel rather than by courier.** *(deferred)*
C0's only rotation path is re-enrollment. The successor is renewal over the existing mTLS channel: a node authenticates with its current credential and receives the next one. What differs between the candidates is **who produced the material** — the operator by courier, the operator offline with the coordinator relaying, an online issuer, or the node itself — and not how it is delivered, which is the mgmt channel in every case but the first.
*Because* re-enrollment is the **least secure delivery path this design has**, and it is currently the only rotation path: the mgmt channel is mutually authenticated, integrity-protected and ours, while the courier channel is email, a chat app, or a stick in a pocket. Any rotation riding the mgmt channel is an improvement on the mechanism it replaces.
*Accepting* the property C0 owes it, which is cheap now and expensive later: **a principal's `identity/principals/<leaf>.crt|.key` must be replaceable on its own**, without touching the artifact set or any other principal's material. Nothing may come to depend on the identity store changing only as a whole.
*Revisit* with a credential spent at first use — the operator minting two credentials offline, the bundle carrying only the first, and the second **encrypted to the node's current public key** and relayed by the coordinator as opaque bytes, deleted on fetch so a second attempt fails visibly. That composition needs no online signing authority and gives an attacker-first race a detectable outcome. It also states a rule this design was missing: **an artifact needs integrity and authenticity, which a signature gives it; a credential needs confidentiality as well**, because the coordinator sits in the path and is not a party to the exchange.

**ENRL-C0-28. Bound a hot issuer by chaining credentials, not by restricting it.** *(deferred)*
Nothing automates issuance in C0. When something does, the shape is a hierarchy rather than a choice: the cold CA issues intermediates; a hot central intermediate issues node principals and per-node intermediates; a per-node intermediate issues that node's own leaves. A node-scoped intermediate is free — an attacker who takes the node already holds those keys — and must not cover the node's own principal, or a node renews its own identity forever. What bounds the central one is **continuity**: a new credential's public key is signed by its **predecessor's private key**, and a peer with history requires that link. **A ladder-bound issuer is one that never holds the private key of what it certifies** — which is a property of that tier alone, not of issuers generally: a node-scoped intermediate lives where those keys live, deliberately, and is therefore outside the ladder rather than in violation of it.
*Because* the issuer never certifies continuity, only the binding of a key to a name — so a hijacked issuer can mint a certificate carrying no continuity proof, which peers who have met that principal reject. The mechanism never classifies issuers; it classifies certificates. **For a pair with history the issuer stops being something that must be trusted and becomes something that must merely be available**, which is how a hot issuer joins the rule the rest of this infrastructure already obeys: *infrastructure failure is availability failure, never authority failure.*
*Accepting* three things. Continuity is **opportunistic**: a peer with no history falls back to the anchor, so first contact is trust-on-first-use and coverage grows with the age of the rete. The ladder must be **ordered**, with peers rejecting a credential at or behind the position they have seen, or a hijacked issuer replays a genuine older link. And **peers hold the ladder state, never the issuer**, or hijacking it rewrites the history the property rests on. A legitimate discontinuity is announced through the cold mgmt path, so an unannounced one is an alarm — which is the recovery channel whose absence killed public-key pinning on the web.
*Revisit* with the threat decomposition that scopes it: a leaked node yields an attacker who can **fork** a ladder but not follow it, since they cannot predict a key the node's own generator has yet to produce — so a fork is provable misbehaviour. A hijacked issuer cannot even fork. A node under an attacker's continuing control is defeated by none of it, per [ENRL-C0-26](#decision-log).
*Rejected* restricting what an issuer may sign as the primary bound — "re-issue only" does not help when re-issuing the operator's own identity owns the rete, which is exactly what re-issue is for.

### What must not be foreclosed

**ENRL-C0-29. Let a credential arrive by means other than a bundle.** *(deferred)*
Nothing may assume the bundle format is the only way a credential reaches a node, and the courier and machine-to-machine forms need not be the same one.
*Because* a bundle is a file a human handles once and a rotation credential is a payload over mTLS; sharing a format buys code and pays in a dependency between contexts that will diverge.
*Revisit* with encryption, which is the hardening that works where an expiry did not. The likely shape encrypts only the **secret entries**, leaving the container readable — so the manifest, the trust domain and the anchors' fingerprints can be verified and displayed **before** a recipient is asked for a secret, and a re-enrollment needs no secret at all, the node's existing public key being the recipient key. Deliberately not settled here.

**ENRL-C0-30. Keep the anchor separable and the issuer replaceable.** *(deferred)*
Exactly one thing is common to a fleet — the **trust anchor** — while credentials are per principal and artifact sets per node. So a two-phase provisioning split moves one field, and `flor install` gaining an optional anchor argument is the whole of its eventual shape. Separately, the rete CA is one implementation of a boundary rather than a permanent fixture: **the identity store is a sink, and nothing may assume what filled it.** No code may require that a certificate came from `retectl`, only that it validates against the anchor and matches its principal.
*Because* a cloud deployment, a federated anchor, a fleet image and an external issuer are four callers for the same two properties, and both cost nothing today. The structural fit for the last is already present: this design's agent occupies the position SPIRE's agent does — supervising workloads, holding per-scope durable state, delivering their material — so consuming an external issuer means the agent fetching from one instead of from the coordinator, not a new component.
*Accepting* that a credential may one day be created by a component this project does not ship, which is where an open-core boundary would fall — and that it needs no plugin mechanism, since the extension shape here is a supervised workload: a separate executable, a `run` name, an opaque payload.

**ENRL-C0-31. Name the coordinator, never its address.** *(deferred)*
`agent.json`'s `sync` block names the coordinator by **principal**, and the dial resolves through the compiler-projected `inbounds` map. Nothing in a bundle or an artifact may carry the coordinator's address as the primary fact.
*Because* from the milestone where a node joins at a link to *some* node, its position is not known when its material is issued — so the address cannot be written into a credential, and the name can. The eventual answer has two complementary halves: any node relaying, so a joiner needs *a* path rather than *the* address; and delegated introduction, since whoever admits a node is the one party guaranteed to have a working path and can hand over a **verified** one rather than a claimed one.

**ENRL-C0-32. Admit foreign and temporary principals without new artifact semantics.** *(deferred)*
An `ingress.allow` entry names a full SPIFFE ID, which carries its trust domain, so a **foreign** principal is already expressible — and an entry must never be shortened to a bare local name. A **temporary** grant is a short-lived identity rather than a time-boxed permission.
*Because* a guest is a member with a short lease while two operators' devices meeting are two trust domains interoperating without either joining, and collapsing them is the mistake to avoid. And a time-boxed ACL entry would need validity windows in the compiled artifact **and** clock agreement between every enforcing pair — a distributed-time problem introduced to express what a credential lifetime already expresses.
*Accepting* that making an ACL entry time-valued must remain an **additive** payload change, which is the same room a per-principal revocation floor needs, so one field's worth of space serves both.
*Rejected* nothing here for the service-mesh deployment, which for enrollment is a strict subset of the workspace case — always-connected, unattended, operator-owned nodes — and stresses cadence and issuer choice rather than any part of this page.

**ENRL-C0-38. Treat a transition between two sound states as unsound, and rely on topology rather than on a mechanism.** *(deferred)*
Nodes converge independently and at different speeds, so a rete passes through **mixed states** that neither endpoint state describes. C0 builds nothing for this and does not have to, for two structural reasons rather than by luck. **Application is atomic within a node**: a set arrives in one response, coherent by construction, staged and activated by a pointer flip, so no node ever runs half a change. And **C0 has no relaying**: every connection is initiator-to-target, so no node's reachability depends on a third node's configuration, and a mixed state can only misalign a pair — a caller that updated before its target sees an outage that heals when the target converges. **The exception is self-referential change**: anything altering the path to the coordinator can strand a node that has not yet applied it, since the thing that would tell it has become unreachable. That is the one C0 transition with no safe order, it is already the case [ENRL-C0-21](#decision-log)'s repair path exists for, and the operator's rule is not to move the coordinator in one step.
*Because* both containments are properties of *this* milestone and neither survives contact with the next. C1 makes reachability transitive, at which point a node applying a change first can break the path of a node that has not — and the nodes that lose their path are exactly the ones the fix cannot reach. That is a ctrl-plane concern rather than a mgmt one, and it is a real coordinator's to solve: sequenced delivery, per-node confirmation, and phased application — build the new, confirm it, only then retire the old.
*Accepting* that C0's transitions are as wide as its slowest channel, which for a courier-fed node is days ([ENRL-C0-37](#decision-log)) — so a mixed state is a condition rather than a moment.
*Accepting* that during one, effective access is the **union** across un-converged nodes, which can be more permissive than either endpoint state. For a removal that is [ENRL-C0-24](#decision-log)'s non-atomic revocation seen from the other side.
Two properties are owed to the successor. **A node's reachability must be expressible as a set during a transition, not a single value** — which makes make-before-break an operator procedure needing no coordination machinery: publish a set naming both the old and the new address, let it converge, then publish the retirement. `inbounds` projects one address per vertex today, so the procedure is not expressible, and reserving the plurality without building the rotation is exactly what `anchors.pem` does for trust. And **nothing may assume the per-node version sequences are the only ordering fact**: they are deliberately independent and nothing compares them across nodes, so a phased rollout needs a rete-wide epoch *above* them, arriving additively and shaped as `obeys_mgmt_version` is shaped. C1's commit-timeout and pending-confirm record are the seed, per-node "did it apply" being the precondition for any phasing.
*Rejected* building ordering or phasing into C0, whose topology makes it unnecessary and whose only self-referential change has an out-of-band repair; and treating the coordinator-move case as a staleness problem, which is how it currently reads and which hides that it is the one transition the design cannot make safely.

### Certificate lifetimes

**ENRL-C0-33. Warn from whatever touches a certificate.**
Every component that reads a certificate checks its validity window and warns inside the last **20% of that certificate's total validity, or 14 days, whichever is longer** — 18 days on a 90-day leaf, about ten weeks on a 365-day signer, two years on a 3650-day root. The obligation follows what each component already does: **`flor enroll`** writes the anchors and every principal's certificate, so it warns on all of them; **`flor agent`** reads the anchors and validates each envelope's signer certificate at admission, warning on those and reading public certificates only, never a key; **`flor-vertex`** alone loads a TLS leaf, so leaf expiry is its warning; **`retectl`** touches the root CA at every `issue-bundle` and the signer at every `compile`. Neither number is configurable.
*Because* distributing the work by what a component already reads needs no registry of who watches what, and puts the warning nearest the material. The placement that matters most falls out for free: `flor enroll` fires while a human is watching and `retectl` fires where the operator is standing, which answers "who is in a position to act" for the two certificates only the operator can replace — without building C1's issuance ledger.
*Because* one constant is right for a leaf and absurd for the other two; a fortnight's notice on a ten-year root is no notice. **The fraction carries the scale and the floor carries the courier**: 20% keeps the warning proportionate, while 14 days is the slowest remedy C0 has — notice, cut a bundle, deliver out of band, enroll. Both are derived from material the checker already holds, which is why neither is a setting.
*Accepting* that **none of this binds anything**: a log line can be missed, and C0's real mechanism is the operator's calendar against a known ninety days. What the design owes the gap is noise from four directions rather than a control it has not built.
*Accepting* that both numbers are placeholders with stated criteria, as the 90-day default is, and that a certificate shorter than seventy days warns from its floor rather than its fraction — pathological in a milestone whose shortest category is ninety.
*Revisit* with the operator-side issuance ledger of [ENRL-C0-24](#decision-log), which replaces warnings with a list.
*Rejected* a configurable threshold, where the certificate-lifetime control C0 declines to build would start; per-category thresholds, the same knob wearing a table; a single constant, on the scale argument above; and the agent as sole emitter, which puts the warning furthest from the person who can act.
